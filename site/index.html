Statistical Inference — Course Notes Casella & Berger as the mathematical spine, taught computationally

A measure-theoretic, proof-driven walk through Casella & Berger (2nd ed.), with every major result tied to a seeded simulation and real-world intuition. Written for a reader comfortable through real analysis and measure-theoretic probability.

How these notes work

  • Rigor. Full proofs at the level of someone who has done measure theory; the measure-theoretic scaffolding (product measures, pushforwards, sub-σ-algebras) is made explicit rather than hidden.
  • Computation. Each major claim ships with a Simulation Check box reporting real output from a seeded, pure-Python standard-library simulation — empirical quantities next to their theoretical targets.
  • Intuition. Why each idea matters, where it breaks, and how it shows up in quantitative / ML / experimentation work.

How to read these notes — caveats

These notes were drafted by an AI assistant (Kiro) working interactively, and these are the limitations a reader should know about up front. Where a specific claim is contested or unverified, it carries an inline ⚠ Flag box on the page itself.

  • Cross-check against the book. Every proof here was written out rather than copied, which means every proof is also a place an error could have entered. The book is the authority; these notes are a companion to it. Section numbering follows C&B 2nd edition.
  • The LaTeX twins have never been compiled. No TeX engine was available in the environment where they were written, so each chNN.tex is unverified source. Expect to fix small errors on first compile (Overleaf is the easy path).
  • The rendered pages were never viewed by their author. The authoring environment had no browser and no outside network access. Layout, MathJax rendering, and overflow were checked by script (tag balance, link resolution, estimated equation widths), not by eye.
  • Simulation numbers come from single seeded runs. Each Simulation Check box reports real output — nothing is invented — but Monte Carlo error typically affects the last one or two digits shown. Theoretical targets are printed alongside precisely so you can judge the agreement. Every script and its saved output live in simulations/ in the repository; re-running reproduces the quoted figures exactly.
  • Everything is pure-standard-library Python. No NumPy or SciPy was available, so distributions, quantiles, and the incomplete beta function are implemented from scratch in simulations/statlib.py. That module self-checks against published table values, but it is not a substitute for a tested numerical library.
  • Simulations illustrate, they do not prove. A matching simulation is evidence that a derivation was transcribed correctly, not that a theorem is true. The proofs carry the argument; the numbers are a sanity check on them.

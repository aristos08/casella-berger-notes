Chapter 6 — Principles of Data Reduction Sufficiency, ancillarity, completeness, the likelihood principle, and equivariance

Chapter 5 asked what a statistic's distribution looks like. Chapter 6 asks which statistics are worth computing: when can we throw data away without losing anything about $\theta$, what must we keep, and what constraints should a sensible procedure respect?

§6.1 Introduction

A statistic $T$ partitions the sample space: $A_t=\{x:T(x)=t\}$. An analyst who reports only $T(x)=t$ has forgotten which point of $A_t$ occurred. In measure-theoretic terms, $T$ is a measurable map and reducing the data to $T$ means retaining only the sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\sigma(T)\subset\mathcal B^n$. Three principles say when that forgetting is harmless or required:

  • Sufficiency principle. Compress to a statistic that loses no information about $\theta$.
  • Likelihood principle. The data affect inference about $\theta$ only through the likelihood function.
  • Equivariance principle. A procedure should respect the symmetries of the problem (units, shifts, relabelings).
Why this matters. Every running total, rollup table, or fixed-size feature vector computed from a stream is a data-reduction decision. Sufficiency tells you exactly when that is lossless under a given model (and the qualifier matters: see the A/B-test caveat in §6.2.1). Ancillarity and the likelihood principle explain why the "right" reference set for an error rate is sometimes the experiment you actually ran rather than the one you imagined. Equivariance explains why a method should not depend on whether you measured in meters or feet.

§6.2 The Sufficiency Principle

§6.2.1 Sufficient Statistics

Definition (sufficiency). A statistic $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T(X)$ does not depend on $\theta$. Precisely: there is a Markov kernel $Q(B\mid t)$ such that, for every $\theta$ and Borel $B$, $Q(B\mid T)$ is a version of $P_\theta(X\in B\mid T)$. (On Borel spaces regular conditional distributions exist, so the kernel is not an extra assumption.)

Discrete criterion. If $X$ is discrete and $q(t\mid\theta)=P_\theta(T=t)$, then $P_\theta(X=x\mid T=T(x))=\dfrac{p(x\mid\theta)}{q(T(x)\mid\theta)}$. So $T$ is sufficient iff this ratio is free of $\theta$ for every $x$.

Factorization Theorem. Let $X$ have pmf/pdf $f(x\mid\theta)$. $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ iff there exist functions $g(t\mid\theta)$ and $h(x)$ with $$f(x\mid\theta)=g\big(T(x)\mid\theta\big)\,h(x)\qquad\text{for all }x,\theta .$$

Proof (discrete case).

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $T$ is sufficient. Put $g(t\mid\theta)=P_\theta(T=t)$ and $h(x)=P(X=x\mid T=T(x))$, which is free of $\theta$. Then $p(x\mid\theta)=P_\theta(T=T(x))\,P(X=x\mid T=T(x))=g(T(x)\mid\theta)h(x)$. ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $p(x\mid\theta)=g(T(x)\mid\theta)h(x)$. Then $q(t\mid\theta)=\sum_{x:T(x)=t}p(x\mid\theta)=g(t\mid\theta)\sum_{x:T(x)=t}h(x)$, so $$\frac{p(x\mid\theta)}{q(T(x)\mid\theta)}=\frac{h(x)}{\sum_{y:T(y)=T(x)}h(y)},$$ which does not involve $\theta$. ∎
The general (measure-theoretic) statement. For a family $\{P_\theta\}$ dominated by a $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu$, the same equivalence holds with $f=dP_\theta/d\mu$ (Halmos–Savage, 1949). The proof reduces to the discrete one using the fact that a dominated family is equivalent to a countable convex combination $P^*=\sum_i c_iP_{\theta_i}$, so that $\frac{dP_\theta}{dP^*}$ is $\sigma(T)$-measurable exactly when $T$ is sufficient. See Lehmann–Romano, Testing Statistical Hypotheses, §2.6. For densities in practice, "$f(x\mid\theta)=g(T(x)\mid\theta)h(x)$" is all you need.

Examples

  • Bernoulli($\theta$). $f(x\mid\theta)=\theta^{t}(1-\theta)^{n-t}\cdot1$ with $t=\sum x_i$, so $T=\sum X_i$.
  • $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2$ known. Using $\sum(x_i-\mu)^2=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2+n(\bar x-\mu)^2$, $$\begin{aligned}f(x\mid\mu)&=\underbrace{e^{-n(\bar x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}_{g(\bar x\mid\mu)}\\ &\quad\times\underbrace{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}e^{-\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}_{h(x)},\end{aligned}$$ so $\bar X$ is sufficient. With $\sigma^2$ also unknown, the same identity gives $(\bar X,S^2)$.
  • Uniform$(0,\theta)$. $f(x\mid\theta)=\theta^{-n}\mathbf 1\{x_{(n)}\le\theta\}\cdot\mathbf 1\{x_{(1)}\ge0\}$, so $X_{(n)}$ is sufficient. The support depends on $\theta$, so this is not an exponential family.
  • Exponential families. If $f(x\mid\theta)=h(x)c(\theta)\exp\{\sum_{i=1}^kw_i(\theta)t_i(x)\}$, then for an iid sample the vector $\big(\sum_jt_1(X_j),\dots,\sum_jt_k(X_j)\big)$ is sufficient, by factorization: $c(\theta)^n\exp\{\sum_iw_i(\theta)T_i\}\cdot\prod_jh(x_j)$.
  • The whole sample, or its order statistics. $T(X)=X$ is trivially sufficient; for iid data the order statistics are too, because $\prod f(x_i)=\prod f(x_{(i)})$.
Two structural facts. (i) Sufficiency is not unique: any bijective function of a sufficient statistic is sufficient. (ii) If $T=r(S)$ and $T$ is sufficient, then $S$ is sufficient (take $g(r(S(x))\mid\theta)$ in the factorization). So sufficiency is inherited by finer statistics, and the interesting question is how coarse one can go (§6.2.2).

Sufficiency means you can regenerate the data

Proposition (regeneration). Let $T$ be sufficient with $\theta$-free kernel $Q(\cdot\mid t)$. Draw $X\sim P_\theta$, compute $T(X)$, then draw $X'\sim Q(\cdot\mid T(X))$ using fresh randomness. Then $X'\sim P_\theta$ for every $\theta$.

Proof.

$P(X'\in B)=E_\theta\big[Q(B\mid T)\big]=E_\theta\big[P_\theta(X\in B\mid T)\big]=P_\theta(X\in B)$. ∎

Interpretation: a statistician holding only $T$ can manufacture a synthetic data set indistinguishable in law from the real one. Any procedure that uses all of $X$ can be matched in distribution by one that uses only $T$ plus an independent coin. This is the engine behind Rao–Blackwell (Chapter 7).

Real-world: what sufficiency does and does not license. In a randomized A/B test with a Bernoulli conversion outcome, $(n,\sum x_i)$ is sufficient for the conversion rate: the click-by-click log adds nothing if the iid Bernoulli model is right. That qualifier is the whole story. If conversion drifts with time (novelty effects), or users repeat, the order, timestamps and covariates are no longer irrelevant. Sufficiency is a property of a statistic relative to a model, not of the data. A related theorem (Pitman–Koopman–Darmois) says that, under regularity (support not depending on $\theta$), only exponential families admit a sufficient statistic whose dimension stays bounded as $n$ grows. That is why Gaussian, Poisson, and logistic-regression models can be fit from fixed-size summaries, while Cauchy or Uniform$(0,\theta)$-type models cannot.

§6.2.2 Minimal Sufficient Statistics

Definition. A sufficient statistic $T$ is minimal sufficient if for every sufficient statistic $S$ there is a function $r$ with $T=r(S)$. It is the coarsest sufficient partition: the maximal lossless compression of the data.
Theorem (criterion for minimal sufficiency). Let $f(x\mid\theta)\gt0$ (for simplicity) and suppose $T$ satisfies: for all $x,y$, $$\frac{f(x\mid\theta)}{f(y\mid\theta)}\ \text{is constant in }\theta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T(x)=T(y).$$ Then $T$ is minimal sufficient.

Proof.

Sufficiency. Choose one representative $x_t$ from each fiber $\{T=t\}$. For any $x$, $T(x_{T(x)})=T(x)$, so by the ($\Leftarrow$) hypothesis the ratio $f(x\mid\theta)/f(x_{T(x)}\mid\theta)=:h(x)$ is free of $\theta$. Then $f(x\mid\theta)=f(x_{T(x)}\mid\theta)\,h(x)=g(T(x)\mid\theta)h(x)$, a factorization. Minimality. Let $S$ be any sufficient statistic: $f(x\mid\theta)=g'(S(x)\mid\theta)h'(x)$. If $S(x)=S(y)$ then $f(x\mid\theta)/f(y\mid\theta)=h'(x)/h'(y)$ is free of $\theta$, so $T(x)=T(y)$ by the ($\Rightarrow$) hypothesis. Hence $T$ is constant on the fibers of $S$, i.e. a function of $S$. ∎

Examples

  • $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, both unknown. Using the sum-of-squares identity, $$\begin{aligned}\frac{f(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)}{f(y\mid\mu,\sigma^2)}=\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[&n(\bar x^2-\bar y^2)-2n\mu(\bar x-\bar y)\\ &+(n-1)(s_x^2-s_y^2)\Big]\Big\}.\end{aligned}$$ This is constant in $\mu$ iff $\bar x=\bar y$, and then constant in $\sigma^2$ iff $s_x^2=s_y^2$. So $(\bar X,S^2)$ is minimal sufficient.
  • Uniform$(\theta,\theta+1)$. The likelihood is $\mathbf 1\{x_{(n)}-1\lt\theta\lt x_{(1)}\}$, the indicator of an interval. Two samples give proportional likelihoods iff they give the same interval, so $(X_{(1)},X_{(n)})$ is minimal sufficient: a two-dimensional statistic for a one-dimensional parameter.
  • Cauchy location. The ratio $\prod_i\frac{1+(y_i-\theta)^2}{1+(x_i-\theta)^2}$ is a ratio of monic polynomials in $\theta$ of degree $2n$, constant iff the multisets $\{x_i\}$ and $\{y_i\}$ agree (compare roots $y_i\pm\mathrm i$ and $x_i\pm\mathrm i$). The order statistics are minimal sufficient: no reduction beyond sorting.
Intuition. The criterion says: group two data sets together exactly when their likelihood functions have the same shape in $\theta$. The minimal sufficient statistic is "the likelihood function up to a constant". This idea reappears as the likelihood principle in §6.3.

§6.2.3 Ancillary Statistics

Definition. A statistic $S(X)$ is ancillary for $\theta$ if its distribution does not depend on $\theta$.
Proposition (location and scale families). (a) Let $X_i=\theta+Z_i$ with $Z_i$ iid from a fixed law. Any statistic with $W(x+c\mathbf 1)=W(x)$ for all $c$ (translation invariant) is ancillary: the range, $S^2$, the residuals $X_i-\bar X$. (b) Let $X_i=\sigma Z_i$. Any $W$ with $W(cx)=W(x)$ for all $c\gt0$ (scale invariant) is ancillary: $X_1/X_2$, $X_1/\sum X_j$, sample skewness.

Proof.

(a) $W(X)=W(\theta\mathbf 1+Z)=W(Z)$, whose law does not involve $\theta$. (b) $W(X)=W(\sigma Z)=W(Z)$. ∎

Ancillary does not mean useless: it tells you how precise you are

Example (Uniform$(\theta,\theta+1)$). Let $R=X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}$ (ancillary) and $\hat\theta=\tfrac12\big(X_{(1)}+X_{(n)}\big)-\tfrac12$. Given $R=r$, $X_{(1)}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\theta,\theta+1-r)$, so $$\begin{aligned}\hat\theta-\theta\;\Big|\;R=r\ &\sim\ \mathrm{Uniform}\Big(-\tfrac{1-r}{2},\,\tfrac{1-r}{2}\Big),\\ E\big[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\mid R=r\big]&=\frac{(1-r)^2}{12}.\end{aligned}$$

Proof.

The joint density of $(X_{(1)},X_{(n)})=(u,v)$ is $n(n-1)(v-u)^{n-2}$ on $\theta\lt u\lt v\lt\theta+1$. Change variables to $(u,r)$ with $r=v-u$: the density $n(n-1)r^{n-2}$ does not depend on $u$, and $u$ ranges over $(\theta,\theta+1-r)$. So given $R=r$, $u$ is uniform on that interval. Finally $\hat\theta-\theta=(u-\theta)+\tfrac r2-\tfrac12$. ∎

The ancillary $R$ carries no information about where $\theta$ is, but it determines how well we can locate it: when $R$ is near $1$ the interval $(X_{(n)}-1,X_{(1)})$ is almost a point. Reporting a single unconditional MSE averages over lucky and unlucky samples; the relevant precision is the conditional one.

Real-world: ancillaries you already condition on. In regression the design matrix is treated as fixed: its law does not involve the regression coefficients, so it is ancillary and one analyzes conditionally on it. If the achieved sample size is random (survey nonresponse, users who happen to show up), its law typically does not involve the effect of interest, so inference is conditional on the sample size you actually got. This is the conditionality principle of §6.3 in everyday use.

§6.2.4 Sufficient, Ancillary, and Complete Statistics

Definition (completeness). Let $T\sim f_T(\cdot\mid\theta)$. The family $\{f_T(\cdot\mid\theta)\}$ is complete if $$\begin{aligned}&E_\theta[g(T)]=0\ \text{ for all }\theta\\ &\qquad\Longrightarrow\ P_\theta\big(g(T)=0\big)=1\ \text{ for all }\theta .\end{aligned}$$ We say $T$ is a complete statistic. Interpretation: no non-trivial function of $T$ is an unbiased estimator of zero.

Examples

  • Binomial$(n,\theta)$ is complete. $E_\theta g(T)=(1-\theta)^n\sum_kg(k)\binom nkr^k$ with $r=\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}$. If this vanishes for all $\theta\in(0,1)$ it is a polynomial in $r$ vanishing on $(0,\infty)$, so every coefficient $g(k)\binom nk$ is $0$.
  • $X_{(n)}$ for Uniform$(0,\theta)$ is complete. $E_\theta g(T)=\theta^{-n}\int_0^\theta g(t)\,nt^{n-1}dt=0$ for all $\theta$ says the signed measure $\nu(B)=\int_Bg(t)t^{n-1}dt$ vanishes on every $[0,\theta]$. These sets form a $\pi$-system generating the Borel sets of $(0,\infty)$, so $\nu\equiv0$, hence $g=0$ a.e.
  • Full-rank exponential families. If the natural parameter space contains an open set of $\mathbb R^k$, the natural sufficient statistic $\big(\sum t_1(X_j),\dots,\sum t_k(X_j)\big)$ is complete, by uniqueness of the (bilateral) Laplace transform.
Proposition (when completeness fails). If $T$ has a non-constant function $r(T)$ that is ancillary with finite mean, then $T$ is not complete.

Proof.

Let $c=E[r(T)]$, the same for all $\theta$ by ancillarity. Then $g(T)=r(T)-c$ satisfies $E_\theta g(T)=0$ for every $\theta$, yet $P_\theta(g(T)\ne0)\gt0$. ∎

Another non-example: the $N(\theta,\theta^2)$ family. Here $(\bar X,S^2)$ is minimal sufficient (a curved exponential family: two statistics, one parameter), but $E[\bar X^2]=\theta^2(1+\tfrac1n)$ and $E[S^2]=\theta^2$, so $g=\bar X^2-(1+\tfrac1n)S^2$ has mean $0$ for every $\theta$ without being zero. Minimal sufficient statistics need not be complete.
Basu's Theorem. If $T$ is a complete sufficient statistic for $\theta$ and $S$ is ancillary, then $S$ and $T$ are independent (under every $\theta$).

Proof.

Fix an event $A$ for $S$. Ancillarity: $p_A:=P_\theta(S\in A)$ is free of $\theta$. Sufficiency: $\psi_A(t):=P_\theta(S\in A\mid T=t)=Q(\{x:S(x)\in A\}\mid t)$ is free of $\theta$, since it is computed from the $\theta$-free kernel $Q$. Then $$E_\theta\big[\psi_A(T)-p_A\big]=P_\theta(S\in A)-p_A=0\quad\text{for all }\theta .$$ By completeness, $\psi_A(T)=p_A$ almost surely for every $\theta$, i.e. $P_\theta(S\in A\mid T)=P_\theta(S\in A)$ a.s. As $A$ was arbitrary, $S\perp T$. ∎

Three applications of Basu

  1. $\bar X\perp S^2$ for normal samples (Thm 5.3.1(b), reproved). For $N(\mu,\sigma_0^2)$ with $\sigma_0^2$ fixed, $\bar X$ is complete and sufficient for $\mu$ (full-rank exponential family), and $S^2=\frac{\sigma_0^2}{n-1}\sum(Z_i-\bar Z)^2$ with $Z_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma_0$ has a law free of $\mu$, so it is ancillary. Basu gives $\bar X\perp S^2$ for each fixed $\sigma_0^2$, hence for all $(\mu,\sigma^2)$. No orthogonal matrices needed, and the proof shows where normality enters: through completeness of $\bar X$. (The Chapter 5 simulation with Exponential data has no parameter family in which $\bar X$ is complete and $S^2$ ancillary, so Basu does not apply, and indeed $\mathrm{corr}(\bar X,S^2)=0.70$.)
  2. Exponential scale family: total vs. share. For $X_i\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\text{scale }\theta)$, $T=\sum X_i$ is complete sufficient and $W=X_1/T$ is scale-invariant, hence ancillary. So $W\perp T$, and $W\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,n-1)$ by the Gamma–Beta relation of §5.3. The share of the total contributed by one unit is independent of the total.
  3. Uniform$(0,\theta)$: max vs. ratio. $X_{(n)}$ is complete sufficient and $X_{(1)}/X_{(n)}$ is scale-invariant, so they are independent.

Theorem (Bahadur): complete sufficient implies minimal sufficient. If a minimal sufficient statistic exists, then every complete sufficient statistic is minimal sufficient.

Proof.

Let $T$ be complete sufficient and $U$ minimal sufficient. By minimality $U=r(T)$ for some $r$. Replacing $T$ by a bounded bijective image (e.g. $\arctan T$) we may assume $T$ is bounded. Put $\psi(U)=E[T\mid U]$, which is free of $\theta$ because $U$ is sufficient. Then $T-\psi(U)=T-\psi(r(T))$ is a function of $T$ with $E_\theta[T-\psi(U)]=0$ for all $\theta$. By completeness $T=\psi(U)$ a.s., so $T$ is a function of $U$. Thus $T$ and $U$ are functions of each other, so $T$ is minimal sufficient. ∎

§6.3 The Likelihood Principle

§6.3.1 The Likelihood Function

Definition. Given the observed $x$, the likelihood function is $L(\theta\mid x)=f(x\mid\theta)$, viewed as a function of $\theta$ with $x$ held fixed. It is not a density in $\theta$; it does not integrate to $1$, and only ratios $L(\theta_1\mid x)/L(\theta_2\mid x)$ are meaningful because any positive multiplicative constant depending on $x$ alone is arbitrary.
Proposition (minimal sufficiency = likelihood shape). Fix $\theta_0$ and define $T(x)$ to be the function $\theta\mapsto f(x\mid\theta)/f(x\mid\theta_0)$ (assume positivity). Then $T$ is minimal sufficient.

Proof.

$f(x\mid\theta)/f(y\mid\theta)$ is constant in $\theta$ iff $f(x\mid\theta)/f(x\mid\theta_0)=f(y\mid\theta)/f(y\mid\theta_0)$ for all $\theta$, i.e. iff $T(x)=T(y)$. Apply the criterion of §6.2.2. ∎

Two experiments, one likelihood

A coin is flipped repeatedly and the result is $9$ heads and $3$ tails. Two statisticians had different plans: (A) flip exactly $12$ times; (B) flip until the third tail appears. Their likelihoods for $p=P(\text{heads})$ are $$L_A(p)=\binom{12}{9}p^9(1-p)^3,\qquad L_B(p)=\binom{11}{2}p^9(1-p)^3 .$$ They are proportional (ratio $\binom{12}{9}/\binom{11}{2}=220/55=4$ for every $p$). Yet for $H_0:p=\tfrac12$ versus $H_1:p\gt\tfrac12$ the one-sided $p$-values differ: $$\begin{aligned}p_A&=P\big(\mathrm{Bin}(12,\tfrac12)\ge9\big)=\frac{299}{4096}=0.0730,\\ p_B&=P\big(\mathrm{Bin}(11,\tfrac12)\ge9\big)=\frac{67}{2048}=0.0327 .\end{aligned}$$ (For B: at least $9$ heads before the third tail iff at most $2$ tails in the first $11$ flips.) At $\alpha=0.05$ one design rejects and the other does not, from the same data.

§6.3.2 The Formal Likelihood Principle

Write $\mathrm{Ev}(E,x)$ for "the evidence about $\theta$ arising from experiment $E$ with outcome $x$". Three principles:

Formal Sufficiency Principle (FSP). If $T$ is sufficient in $E$ and $T(x)=T(y)$, then $\mathrm{Ev}(E,x)=\mathrm{Ev}(E,y)$.
Conditionality Principle (CP). Let $E_1,E_2$ be experiments on the same $\theta$. Let $E^*$ pick $J\in\{1,2\}$ with known probabilities not depending on $\theta$, run $E_J$, and report $(J,X_J)$. Then $\mathrm{Ev}(E^*,(j,x_j))=\mathrm{Ev}(E_j,x_j)$. (Analyze the experiment actually performed, not the coin toss that chose it.)
Formal Likelihood Principle (LP). Let $E_1,E_2$ be experiments on the same $\theta$ with outcomes $x_1^*,x_2^*$ such that $L_2(\theta\mid x_2^*)=C\,L_1(\theta\mid x_1^*)$ for all $\theta$ and some constant $C\gt0$. Then $\mathrm{Ev}(E_1,x_1^*)=\mathrm{Ev}(E_2,x_2^*)$.
Birnbaum's Theorem (1962). The FSP and the CP together imply the LP.

Proof (discrete case).

Let $E^*$ toss a fair coin to choose $E_1$ or $E_2$, so $f^*((j,x)\mid\theta)=\tfrac12f_j(x\mid\theta)$. Define a statistic $T$ on $E^*$ by $T(1,x_1^*)=T(2,x_2^*)=(1,x_1^*)$ and $T(j,x)=(j,x)$ otherwise. $T$ is sufficient. At the merged point, $P_\theta\big((1,x_1^*)\mid T=(1,x_1^*)\big)=\dfrac{\tfrac12f_1(x_1^*\mid\theta)}{\tfrac12f_1(x_1^*\mid\theta)+\tfrac12Cf_1(x_1^*\mid\theta)}=\dfrac{1}{1+C}$, free of $\theta$; elsewhere $T$ is injective, so the conditional law is a point mass. By the FSP, $\mathrm{Ev}(E^*,(1,x_1^*))=\mathrm{Ev}(E^*,(2,x_2^*))$. By the CP, $\mathrm{Ev}(E^*,(1,x_1^*))=\mathrm{Ev}(E_1,x_1^*)$ and $\mathrm{Ev}(E^*,(2,x_2^*))=\mathrm{Ev}(E_2,x_2^*)$. Chaining these gives $\mathrm{Ev}(E_1,x_1^*)=\mathrm{Ev}(E_2,x_2^*)$. ∎
Consequences and controversy. Under the LP the stopping rule is irrelevant once the data are in hand: both designs above must report the same evidence. A Bayesian analysis obeys this automatically: with a $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ prior, both designs produce the posterior $\mathrm{Beta}(a+9,b+3)$. Frequentist error rates (like the $p$-values above) are computed over the sample space, which depends on the design, so they violate the LP. Frequentists reply that controlling error rates is the point, and that therefore the design is relevant. The Birnbaum argument itself is contested in the literature (see Mayo, Statistical Science 2014 for a critique and the replies it provoked), so treat the LP as a position with a famous proof, not as a settled fact.
Flag — Birnbaum's theorem is genuinely disputed. I have presented the standard discrete-case proof, and it is the one found in C&B. But the claim that it establishes the likelihood principle has been attacked in the literature — most prominently by Deborah Mayo (Statistical Science, 2014), who argues the argument equivocates on what the sufficiency and conditionality principles are allowed to mean, with published replies defending it. I am not in a position to adjudicate that dispute, and this page does not. Read the proof as a precise statement about a particular formalization of "evidence", not as a demonstration that frequentist practice is incoherent.

Why this matters: optional stopping ("peeking")

Suppose you monitor an A/B test, looking at $z_i=S_i/\sqrt i$ after every observation and stop the first time $|z_i|\gt1.96$. Under $H_0$ the law of the iterated logarithm says $|S_i|/\sqrt i$ has $\limsup=\infty$ almost surely, so with unlimited looks you will eventually reject with probability $1$. The nominal 5% is a property of one pre-specified look, not of a procedure that peeks.

Simulation check — type I error under peeking

$H_0$ true, data $N(0,1)$, a z-test at nominal 5%, at most $100$ observations, $40{,}000$ reps:

one look at n=100:                    P(reject) = 0.0499
10 equally spaced looks (10,...,100): P(reject) = 0.1958
100 looks (after every observation):  P(reject) = 0.3745

Peeking after every observation multiplies the false-positive rate by about $7.5$ ($0.3745$ vs $0.0499$) with only $100$ observations. Remedies: pre-register a fixed sample size, or use a sequential design with a corrected boundary (alpha-spending, group-sequential tests, always-valid p-values). The LP viewpoint offers another: report the likelihood or posterior, which do not depend on how you decided to stop.

§6.4 The Equivariance Principle

Measurement equivariance and the formal principle

Estimate the mean temperature of a reactor. Whether the thermometer reads in Celsius or Fahrenheit, your answer should agree after converting units. This is measurement equivariance. Formalizing: let $\mathcal G$ be a group of bijections $g$ of the sample space.

Definition (invariant model, induced group). The family $\{f(\cdot\mid\theta)\}$ is invariant under $\mathcal G$ if for each $g\in\mathcal G$ and $\theta$ there is a $\bar g(\theta)\in\Theta$ such that $Y=g(X)$ has law $f(\cdot\mid\bar g(\theta))$ whenever $X\sim f(\cdot\mid\theta)$. The maps $\bar g$ form the induced group $\bar{\mathcal G}$ on $\Theta$.
Equivariance Principle. If $Y=g(X)$ then inference about $\bar g(\theta)$ based on $y$ should be the "same" as inference about $\theta$ based on $x$, transported by $\bar g$. For a point estimator $W$ this reads $$W\big(g(x)\big)=\bar g\big(W(x)\big)\qquad\text{for all }g\in\mathcal G,\ x .$$ Such $W$ is called equivariant.

Examples

  • Location group $g_a(x)=x+a\mathbf 1$, $\bar g_a(\theta)=\theta+a$. Equivariant: $W(x+a\mathbf 1)=W(x)+a$. Satisfied by $\bar x$, the median, the midrange, trimmed means. Not satisfied by $0.8\,\bar x$ (it shrinks toward $0$, a fixed point that the group moves).
  • Scale group $g_c(x)=cx$ ($c\gt0$), $\bar g_c(\sigma)=c\sigma$. Equivariant estimators of $\sigma$: $W(cx)=cW(x)$, e.g. $S$, the mean absolute deviation.
  • Location-scale group $x\mapsto ax+b\mathbf 1$: estimators of $\mu$ satisfy $W(ax+b)=aW(x)+b$; estimators of $\sigma$ satisfy $W(ax+b)=|a|W(x)$.
Proposition (constant risk). Suppose the model is invariant and the loss is invariant: $L(\bar g\theta,\bar ga)=L(\theta,a)$. If $W$ is equivariant, its risk $R(\theta,W)=E_\theta L(\theta,W(X))$ is constant on orbits of $\bar{\mathcal G}$. In particular, for a location model with $L=\ell(a-\theta)$, $R(\theta,W)=E_0\,\ell(W(Z))$ for every $\theta$.

Proof.

Let $Y=gX$ with $X\sim f(\cdot\mid\theta)$, so $Y\sim f(\cdot\mid\bar g\theta)$. Then $$\begin{aligned}R(\bar g\theta,W)&=E_{\bar g\theta}L\big(\bar g\theta,W(Y)\big)=E_\theta L\big(\bar g\theta,W(gX)\big)\\ &=E_\theta L\big(\bar g\theta,\bar gW(X)\big)=E_\theta L\big(\theta,W(X)\big)\\ &=R(\theta,W).\end{aligned}$$ For the location case, $W(Z+\theta\mathbf 1)-\theta=W(Z)$. ∎

Constant risk is what makes the problem tractable: instead of comparing risk functions, we compare single numbers, and a best equivariant estimator can exist even when no uniformly best estimator does. For squared error in a location family it is the Pitman estimator, $$W^*(x)=\frac{\int\theta\prod_if(x_i-\theta)\,d\theta}{\int\prod_if(x_i-\theta)\,d\theta},$$ the posterior mean under a flat prior, which equals $\bar x$ for normal data. We return to it in Chapter 7.

Simulation check — constant risk for equivariant estimators

$N(\theta,1)$, $n=10$, $120{,}000$ reps per $\theta$. MSE of three estimators of $\theta$:

theta   MSE[mean]   MSE[median]   MSE[0.8*mean]   theory for 0.8*mean = 0.064+(0.2*theta)^2
  0.0    0.0996      0.1371         0.0637          0.0640
  1.0    0.1002      0.1384         0.1050          0.1040
  5.0    0.1000      0.1383         1.0631          1.0640

The mean ($\approx0.100=1/n$) and median ($\approx0.138$) have flat risk, as the proposition predicts. The non-equivariant shrinker $0.8\bar X$ beats both near $\theta=0$ (it is a built-in guess that $\theta\approx0$) and is terrible far from it. Equivariance forbids that kind of hidden favoritism.

Real-world: invariance in practice. Procedures should not depend on arbitrary conventions. The $t$-statistic $\sqrt n\,\bar X/S$ is unchanged by $x\mapsto cx$ ($c\gt0$), so it does not care whether data are in meters or feet; a z-score is invariant under location-scale changes. In machine learning the same idea drives standardization of features and the design of translation-equivariant convolutional layers. The flip side: a method that does depend on units (like regularization on unstandardized predictors) is making an implicit, usually unintended, modeling choice.

Where this leaves you

Chapter 6 gave you three lenses on a data set. Sufficiency (factorization, minimality, and the regeneration picture) says what can be discarded under a model; ancillarity and completeness, tied together by Basu, say what is independent of what and which statistics are uniquely determined; the likelihood principle says that the stopping rule should not matter, a stance that conflicts with error-rate frequentism and explains why peeking is dangerous; equivariance says a procedure should respect the symmetries of the problem, which buys constant risk. Chapter 7 puts these to work: Rao–Blackwell and Lehmann–Scheffé turn sufficient and complete statistics into best unbiased estimators, and MLEs turn out to be functions of the likelihood-shape statistic from §6.3.

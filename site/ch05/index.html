Chapter 5 — Properties of a Random Sample Sums, normal sampling theory, order statistics, convergence, and random generation

The pivot from "a random variable" to "a sample of them." Everything here is the study of the sampling distribution — the law of a statistic $T(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ — which is the object all of frequentist inference manipulates.

§5.1 Basic Concepts of Random Samples

Definition (Random sample, measure-theoretic). $X_1,\dots,X_n$ is a random sample of size $n$ from a population with law $P_X$ on $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B})$ if the $X_i$ are mutually independent and each $X_i \sim P_X$. Equivalently, the joint law on $(\mathbb{R}^n,\mathcal{B}^n)$ is the product measure $P_X^{\otimes n}$. If $P_X$ has density/pmf $f$, the joint density is $\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i)$ — this factorization is the content of the iid assumption, and it is the only thing that makes likelihoods multiply later (Chapter 7).
Definition (Statistic). A statistic $T = T(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is any Borel-measurable function of the sample containing no unknown parameters. $T$ is itself a random variable; its law is the pushforward $P_X^{\otimes n} \circ T^{-1}$, called the sampling distribution.
Why this matters. Frequentist inference rests entirely on computing (or approximating) sampling distributions. "iid" is a modeling idealization. Independence $\Rightarrow$ joint density factorizes $\Rightarrow$ MGFs multiply $\Rightarrow$ tractable sampling distributions; so knowing exactly what it buys tells you what breaks when violated:
  • Time series / panel data: autocorrelation breaks independence; naive standard errors are typically too small (false confidence).
  • Clustered experiments (A/B test randomized by user, analyzed by session): correlated units inflate effective variance.
  • Distribution shift in ML: train/test "iid from the same $P_X$" breaks, so the sampling distribution of test error no longer applies.
  • de Finetti (pointer): exchangeability is strictly weaker than iid and is the natural Bayesian-hierarchical assumption — exchangeable sequences are mixtures of iid sequences.
Treating non-iid data as iid is the single most common applied-statistics error in industry. Keep this lens on throughout.

§5.2 Sums of Random Variables from a Random Sample

Convolution, from first principles

If $X,Y$ are independent with densities $f_X,f_Y$, then $Z=X+Y$ has

$$F_Z(z) = \iint_{x+y\le z} f_X(x)f_Y(y)\,dx\,dy = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,dy,$$

and differentiating under the integral (dominated convergence, since $|\partial_z F_X(z-y)| = f_X(z-y)$ is integrable against $f_Y$) gives $f_Z = f_X * f_Y$. Correct but painful; the MGF is the workhorse.

Fact (MGF of a sum of independent variables). If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are independent, $M_{\sum X_i}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t)$.

Proof.

$M_{\sum X_i}(t) = E[\prod_i e^{tX_i}] = \prod_i E[e^{tX_i}]$, the second step being the defining factorization property of independence (Fubini/Tonelli on the product measure). ∎

With the uniqueness theorem for MGFs (agreement on a neighborhood of $0$ $\Rightarrow$ equal laws), this yields reproductive properties for free:

iid componentssum $\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$
Bernoulli$(p)$Binomial$(n,p)$
Poisson$(\lambda)$Poisson$(n\lambda)$
Gamma$(\alpha,\beta)$ [Exp $=$ Gamma$(1,\beta)$]Gamma$(n\alpha,\beta)$
$N(\mu,\sigma^2)$$N(n\mu,\,n\sigma^2)$

Each is two lines: write $M_{X_i}(t)$, raise to the $n$-th power, recognize the target, invoke uniqueness.

§5.3 Sampling from the Normal Distribution

The chapter's centerpiece: where you earn the right to use $t$- and $F$-tables for life.

§5.3.1 Properties of $\bar X$ and $S^2$

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ iid $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i$, $S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$.

Theorem 5.3.1. (a) $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$.   (b) $\bar X \perp S^2$.   (c) $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$.

(a) is the §5.2 reproductive property scaled by $1/n$. The content is (b),(c), proven together via orthogonal transformation.

Proof of (b) and (c).

Step 1 (standardize). $Z_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$ gives $Z\in\mathbb{R}^n$ with iid $N(0,1)$ coordinates and joint density $f_Z(z)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\|z\|^2/2}$, which depends on $z$ only through $\|z\|^2$: the standard Gaussian measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is rotationally invariant. This is the engine.

Step 2 (rotate). Pick orthogonal $A$ ($AA'=I$) with first row $(\tfrac1{\sqrt n},\dots,\tfrac1{\sqrt n})$ (extend that unit vector to an ONB via Gram–Schmidt). Let $Y=AZ$. Change of variables $z=A'y$ has $|\det A'|=1$ and $\|z\|^2=\|A'y\|^2=y'AA'y=\|y\|^2$, so $f_Y(y)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\|y\|^2/2}$ — identical form. Hence $Y_1,\dots,Y_n$ are themselves iid $N(0,1)$.

Step 3 (identify pieces). $Y_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\sum Z_i=\sqrt n\,\bar Z$, and $\|Y\|^2=\|Z\|^2$, so $$\sum_{i=2}^n Y_i^2 = \sum_i Z_i^2 - n\bar Z^2 = \sum_i(Z_i-\bar Z)^2 = \sum_i\Big(\tfrac{X_i-\bar X}{\sigma}\Big)^2 = \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}$$ ($\mu$ cancels in the centered sum).

Step 4 (conclude).

  • $Y_2,\dots,Y_n$ iid $N(0,1)$ $\Rightarrow \sum_{i=2}^n Y_i^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ (sum of squared standard normals $=$ $\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac{n-1}{2},2)=\chi^2_{n-1}$, a §5.2 reproductive property). This is (c).
  • $Y_1$ depends only on $Y_1$; $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$ depends only on $(Y_2,\dots,Y_n)$. Mutual independence of the $Y_i$ $\Rightarrow$ functions of disjoint blocks are independent (disjoint blocks generate independent sub-σ-algebras). $\bar X$ is an affine function of $Y_1$, so $\bar X\perp S^2$. This is (b).

∎

How special this is. Independence of $\bar X$ and $S^2$ is a fingerprint of normality. Lukács (1942): for iid $X_i$ with finite variance, $\bar X\perp S^2$ only if the $X_i$ are normal. (Weaker, easy: $\mathrm{Cov}(\bar X,S^2)=\mu_3/n$, zero for any symmetric population — necessary, not sufficient.) This previews Basu's theorem (Ch. 6), which explains such independence via completeness of a sufficient statistic.
Simulation check

$n=10$, $N(5,4)$, $300{,}000$ reps:

corr(Xbar, S^2)         = -0.0009     theory: exactly 0
mean[(n-1)S^2/sigma^2]  =  8.985      theory chi2_9 mean = 9
var [(n-1)S^2/sigma^2]  = 18.035      theory chi2_9 var  = 18
var[Xbar]               =  0.4002     theory sigma^2/n   = 0.4000

Control — same experiment with iid $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ instead of normal:

corr(Xbar, S^2) = 0.6992   -- strongly nonzero

The Lukács converse, numerically: drop normality and independence vanishes outright (corr $0.70$).

§5.3.2 Student's $t$ and Snedecor's $F$

Definition (constructions). $$T_p := \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/p}},\quad Z\sim N(0,1),\, W\sim\chi^2_p,\, Z\perp W \;\Rightarrow\; T_p\sim t_p;\qquad F_{p,q} := \frac{U/p}{V/q},\quad U\sim\chi^2_p,\, V\sim\chi^2_q,\, U\perp V.$$
Why this is exactly what inference needs. For iid $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)$ and $\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$, independent. Dividing, $\sigma$ cancels: $$T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}=\frac{(\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}{\sqrt{[(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2]/(n-1)}}\sim t_{n-1}.$$ The $t$-test exists precisely because this is the unique construction producing a pivot (free of $\sigma$) from the two independent pieces of Thm 5.3.1.
Claim (the $t$ density). $f_T(t)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac{p+1}{2})}{\Gamma(p/2)\sqrt{p\pi}}\big(1+\frac{t^2}{p}\big)^{-(p+1)/2}$.

Proof.

Transform $(z,w)\mapsto(t,w)$ via $z=t\sqrt{w/p}$; Jacobian $\partial z/\partial t=\sqrt{w/p}$. Then $$f_T(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi p}\,2^{p/2}\Gamma(p/2)}\int_0^\infty w^{(p-1)/2}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac w2(1+\tfrac{t^2}{p})\Big)dw.$$ With $a=\tfrac12(1+t^2/p)$ the integral is $\Gamma(\tfrac{p+1}{2})a^{-(p+1)/2}$; collecting constants gives the result. ∎
The $F$ density follows identically ($u=\tfrac pq fv$, integrate out $v$): $$f_F(f)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{p+q}{2})}{\Gamma(p/2)\Gamma(q/2)}\Big(\tfrac pq\Big)^{p/2} f^{p/2-1}\Big(1+\tfrac pq f\Big)^{-(p+q)/2}.$$ Moments: $E[T_p]=0$ ($p>1$), $\mathrm{Var}(T_p)=\frac{p}{p-2}$ ($p>2$); $E[F_{p,q}]=\frac{q}{q-2}$ ($q>2$). Heavier tails than Normal/$\chi^2$ — the price of an unknown denominator (not knowing $\sigma$).
Simulation check

$n=10$ (df $9$), $400{,}000$ reps, $T=(\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)$:

empirical mean       =  0.0018    theory 0
empirical var        =  1.2904    theory df/(df-2) = 1.2857
empirical 97.5th pct =  2.2720    table t_(.975,9) = 2.262

$F$ ($U\sim\chi^2_3,V\sim\chi^2_{10}$): mean $1.2497$ vs theory $q/(q-2)=1.25$. And $T_p^2\overset{d}{=}F_{1,p}$: $\mathrm{mean}(T^2)=1.2904$ vs direct $F_{1,9}$ sim $1.2840$ (both theory $1.2857$).

§5.4 Order Statistics

Reorder the sample ascending: $X_{(1)}\le X_{(2)}\le\dots\le X_{(n)}$. These are the raw material of the median, range, quantile estimates, extremes (max load, worst-case latency), and much of nonparametrics.

Theorem (density of a single order statistic). For iid continuous $X_i$ with cdf $F$, density $f$, the $j$-th order statistic has density $$f_{X_{(j)}}(x) = \frac{n!}{(j-1)!\,(n-j)!}\,F(x)^{\,j-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{\,n-j} f(x).$$

Proof (multinomial heuristic made rigorous).

The event $\{X_{(j)}\in(x,x+dx]\}$ requires, up to $o(dx)$, exactly one observation in $(x,x+dx]$, exactly $j-1$ below $x$, and $n-j$ above. By independence and the multinomial coefficient for assigning which observations land where, $$P(X_{(j)}\in(x,x+dx]) = \frac{n!}{(j-1)!\,1!\,(n-j)!} F(x)^{j-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-j} f(x)\,dx + o(dx).$$ Dividing by $dx$ and letting $dx\downarrow 0$ gives the density. (A fully rigorous route differentiates the exact cdf $F_{X_{(j)}}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^n \binom nk F(x)^k(1-F(x))^{n-k}$; the sum telescopes to the stated density.) ∎
The Uniform case is the Rosetta Stone. If $U_i$ iid $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$, then $U_{(j)}\sim \mathrm{Beta}(j,\,n-j+1)$ — read off the density above with $F(u)=u$. Extremes: $U_{(n)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)$ (max), $U_{(1)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,n)$ (min). And via the probability integral transform $U_i=F(X_i)$, every continuous case reduces to the uniform one — so Beta order statistics underlie everything.

§5.5 Convergence Concepts

This section is where measure-theoretic probability earns its keep. Four modes, a strict hierarchy, and the delta method — the asymptotic toolkit all of Chapter 10 runs on.

§5.5.1 Convergence in Probability

$X_n\xrightarrow{P}X$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$, $P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)\to 0$. A statement about the measure of a bad set shrinking, for each tolerance separately.
Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN). If $X_i$ iid with $E[X_1]=\mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2<\infty$, then $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu$.

Proof.

$E[\bar X_n]=\mu$, $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$. Chebyshev: $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon)\le \frac{\sigma^2/n}{\varepsilon^2}\to 0.$ ∎
(The finite-variance assumption is removable — the WLLN holds under just $E|X_1|<\infty$ — but Chebyshev is the honest one-liner.)
Simulation check

$X_i$ iid $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$, $\mu=1$. Empirical $P(|\bar X_n-1|>0.1)$:

 n=   10:  0.7507
 n=   50:  0.4795
 n=  200:  0.1578
 n= 1000:  0.0019
 n= 5000:  0.0000

The bad-set probability marches to $0$, exactly as $\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)=1/(0.01 n)$ predicts as an upper bound.

§5.5.2 Almost Sure Convergence

$X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X$ if $P(\{\omega: X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)\})=1$ — convergence of the sample paths for almost every $\omega$. Strictly stronger than $\xrightarrow{P}$: a.s. $\Rightarrow$ in probability, not conversely.
Strong Law (SLLN), stated. If $X_i$ iid with $E|X_1|<\infty$, then $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu$. (Proof deferred — it needs Kolmogorov's maximal inequality or a truncation argument; not a one-liner.)
Why the distinction is not pedantic. "In probability" says that at any fixed large $n$, you are probably close. "Almost surely" says that if you watch a single run forever, it settles. A long-running estimator, an online learning algorithm, or a Monte Carlo average you keep refining — those are a.s. statements: you care about the one trajectory you are on, not a snapshot.
Simulation check — the typewriter sequence (in probability but NOT a.s.)

On $([0,1],\text{Lebesgue})$, index by sweeps: $n=\tfrac{m(m-1)}2+k$, $A_{m,k}=[\tfrac{k-1}{m},\tfrac km)$, $X_n=\mathbf 1_{A_{m,k}}$. Then $P(A_n)=1/m\to 0$ so $X_n\xrightarrow{P}0$; but every fixed $\omega$ is hit once per sweep, so $X_n(\omega)=1$ infinitely often — no pointwise convergence. Tracking $\omega=0.37$:

sweep m= 1:  n=  1   X_n=1   P(A_n)=1.0000
sweep m= 3:  n=  5   X_n=1   P(A_n)=0.3333
sweep m= 5:  n= 12   X_n=1   P(A_n)=0.2000
sweep m= 7:  n= 24   X_n=1   P(A_n)=0.1429

$X_n(0.37)$ keeps spiking to $1$ forever even as $P(A_n)\to 0$. This is the canonical separation of the two modes.

§5.5.3 Convergence in Distribution

$X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$ if $F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)$ at every continuity point $x$ of $F_X$. The weakest mode — only the laws converge, the variables need not live on a common space. (a.s. $\Rightarrow$ $P$ $\Rightarrow$ $d$; none of the arrows reverse in general.)
Central Limit Theorem. If $X_i$ iid, $E[X_1]=\mu$, $0<\mathrm{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2<\infty$, then $\dfrac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)$.

Proof sketch (characteristic functions).

Let $Y_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$, $\varphi(t)=E[e^{itY_1}]=1-\tfrac{t^2}2+o(t^2)$. The standardized sum has characteristic function $\varphi(t/\sqrt n)^n=\big(1-\tfrac{t^2}{2n}+o(1/n)\big)^n\to e^{-t^2/2}$, the $N(0,1)$ characteristic function. Lévy's continuity theorem converts pointwise convergence of characteristic functions into $\xrightarrow{d}$. ∎
Simulation check — CLT kicking in

The left/right asymmetry at $n=2$ (the exponential's skew) is symmetrized away by $n=50$ — the CLT visibly repairing a skewed summand.

§5.5.4 The Delta Method

Delta method. If $\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $g$ is differentiable at $\theta$ with $g'(\theta)\ne 0$, then $$\sqrt n\big(g(T_n)-g(\theta)\big)\xrightarrow{d}N\!\big(0,\;\sigma^2\,[g'(\theta)]^2\big).$$

Proof.

Taylor: $g(T_n)=g(\theta)+g'(\theta)(T_n-\theta)+R_n$ with $R_n=o(|T_n-\theta|)$. Since $T_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta$ (consistency from the $\sqrt n$-rate), $\sqrt n R_n\xrightarrow{P}0$. Thus $\sqrt n(g(T_n)-g(\theta))=g'(\theta)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)+o_P(1)$, and Slutsky gives the limit $N(0,\sigma^2[g'(\theta)]^2)$. ∎
Why you use this constantly. It is how you get a standard error for any smooth transform of an asymptotically normal estimate — log-odds, ratios, $R^2$, a rate computed from a count — without re-deriving a sampling distribution. It is the mechanical engine behind "SE of a transformed parameter" in every stats package.
Simulation check

$X_i$ iid $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.3)$, $g=\mathrm{logit}$, $g'(p)=\frac1{p(1-p)}=4.762$. Theory: $\sqrt n(\mathrm{logit}\,\bar X-\mathrm{logit}\,p)\to N(0,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2=p(1-p)g'(p)^2=\frac1{p(1-p)}=4.762$.

 n=   50: empirical var = 5.1500   theory = 4.7619
 n=  500: empirical var = 4.7890   theory = 4.7619
 n= 5000: empirical var = 4.7595   theory = 4.7619

Convergence to the delta-method variance is clear; the $n=50$ overshoot is the finite-sample curvature the first-order expansion ignores.

§5.6 Generating a Random Sample

Every simulation above rests on turning uniform random numbers into draws from a target law. Two families: direct (exploit structure) and indirect (accept/reject).

§5.6.1 Direct Methods — the Probability Integral Transform

Inverse-CDF (Smirnov) transform. If $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $F$ is a cdf with generalized inverse $F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge u\}$, then $X=F^{-1}(U)$ has cdf $F$.

Proof.

For continuous strictly increasing $F$: $P(F^{-1}(U)\le x)=P(U\le F(x))=F(x)$, the middle step by monotonicity of $F$. The generalized inverse handles jumps/flats so the identity $\{F^{-1}(u)\le x\}=\{u\le F(x)\}$ holds in general. ∎
Practical reach: exponential ($X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$), and any distribution with a closed-form quantile. Normal has no elementary $F^{-1}$ — hence Box–Muller (a direct method exploiting the 2-D rotational structure of §5.3) or accept/reject.
Simulation check

$\mathrm{Exp}(\text{rate}=2)$ via $X=-\ln(1-U)/2$, $300{,}000$ draws:

empirical mean = 0.5003   theory 1/lam   = 0.5000
empirical var  = 0.2499   theory 1/lam^2 = 0.2500

§5.6.3 The Accept/Reject Algorithm

Accept/Reject. To sample target density $f$ using proposal density $g$ (easy to sample) with $M=\sup_x f(x)/g(x)<\infty$: draw $Y\sim g$, $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ independent; accept $X=Y$ if $U\le \frac{f(Y)}{M\,g(Y)}$, else repeat.

Proof of correctness.

$P(\text{accept}\mid Y=y)=\frac{f(y)}{Mg(y)}$, so for any Borel $B$, $$P(Y\in B,\text{accept})=\int_B \frac{f(y)}{Mg(y)}g(y)\,dy=\frac1M\int_B f(y)\,dy.$$ Taking $B=\mathbb{R}$: $P(\text{accept})=1/M$. Hence $P(X\in B)=P(Y\in B\mid\text{accept})=\int_B f=\;$ target. Each trial accepts independently w.p. $1/M$, so the number of trials is $\mathrm{Geometric}(1/M)$ with mean $M$. ∎
The intuition and the cost. Throw darts under the scaled proposal $M g$; keep those landing under $f$. $M$ is the "wasted area" factor — the mean number of proposals per accepted draw. Choose $g$ hugging $f$ to keep $M$ near $1$. This is the conceptual ancestor of MCMC: when $M$ is uncontrollable (high dimensions), you give up independence and build a Markov chain with $f$ as its stationary law instead.

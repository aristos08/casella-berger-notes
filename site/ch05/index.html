Chapter 5 — Properties of a Random Sample Sums, normal sampling theory, order statistics, convergence, and random generation

The pivot from "a random variable" to "a sample of them." Everything here is the study of the sampling distribution — the law of a statistic $T(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ — which is the object all of frequentist inference manipulates.

§5.1 Basic Concepts of Random Samples

Definition (Random sample, measure-theoretic). $X_1,\dots,X_n$ is a random sample of size $n$ from a population with law $P_X$ on $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B})$ if the $X_i$ are mutually independent and each $X_i \sim P_X$. Equivalently, the joint law on $(\mathbb{R}^n,\mathcal{B}^n)$ is the product measure $P_X^{\otimes n}$. If $P_X$ has density/pmf $f$, the joint density is $\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i)$ — this factorization is the content of the iid assumption, and it is the only thing that makes likelihoods multiply later (Chapter 7).
Definition (Statistic). A statistic $T = T(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is any Borel-measurable function of the sample containing no unknown parameters. $T$ is itself a random variable; its law is the pushforward $P_X^{\otimes n} \circ T^{-1}$, called the sampling distribution.
Why this matters. Frequentist inference rests entirely on computing (or approximating) sampling distributions. "iid" is a modeling idealization. Independence $\Rightarrow$ joint density factorizes $\Rightarrow$ MGFs multiply $\Rightarrow$ tractable sampling distributions; so knowing exactly what it buys tells you what breaks when violated:
  • Time series / panel data: autocorrelation breaks independence; naive standard errors are typically too small (false confidence).
  • Clustered experiments (A/B test randomized by user, analyzed by session): correlated units inflate effective variance.
  • Distribution shift in ML: train/test "iid from the same $P_X$" breaks, so the sampling distribution of test error no longer applies.
  • de Finetti (pointer): exchangeability is strictly weaker than iid and is the natural Bayesian-hierarchical assumption — exchangeable sequences are mixtures of iid sequences.
Treating non-iid data as iid is the single most common applied-statistics error in industry. Keep this lens on throughout.

Population law versus sampling distribution

The population law $P_X$ describes one observation. The sampling distribution describes a function of the whole sample, and it depends on both the statistic and $n$. Take $X_1,\dots,X_n$ iid $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,\theta)$ and $X_{(n)}=\max_i X_i$. Independence gives $P(X_{(n)}\le x)=\prod_i P(X_i\le x)=(x/\theta)^n$ on $[0,\theta]$. That law looks nothing like the uniform: as $n$ grows it piles up against $\theta$. The population is the input; the sampling distribution is the output of the pair (statistic, $n$).

Proposition (sampling without replacement). Draw a simple random sample $X_1,\dots,X_n$ without replacement from a finite population $c_1,\dots,c_N$ with mean $\bar c$ and variance $\sigma^2=\frac1N\sum_k(c_k-\bar c)^2$. The $X_i$ are identically distributed (each uniform on the population) but not independent, and $$E[\bar X]=\bar c,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{N-n}{N-1}.$$

Proof.

Each $X_i$ is uniform on the population, so $E X_i=\bar c$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2$. For $i\ne j$ the pair $(X_i,X_j)$ is uniform over ordered pairs of distinct members, so $$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)&=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{k\ne l}(c_k-\bar c)(c_l-\bar c)\\ &=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\Big[\Big(\sum_k (c_k-\bar c)\Big)^2-\sum_k (c_k-\bar c)^2\Big]=-\frac{\sigma^2}{N-1}.\end{aligned}$$ Therefore $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\big[n\sigma^2-n(n-1)\frac{\sigma^2}{N-1}\big]=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{N-n}{N-1}$. ∎
Why this matters. The factor $\frac{N-n}{N-1}$ is the finite population correction. When the sampled fraction $n/N$ is tiny it is essentially $1$ and "treat it as iid" is harmless. When you sample a large share of a small population (auditing 40% of invoices, polling most of a small town) the iid standard error is too large. So iid errors cut both ways: dependence from clustering inflates variance, dependence from exhausting a population deflates it.
Simulation check

A fixed population of $N=50$ values (drawn once, $\sigma^2=6.4711$), samples of $n=20$, $100{,}000$ reps each:

with replacement   : Var(Xbar) = 0.32456   theory sigma^2/n = 0.32356
without replacement: Var(Xbar) = 0.19707   theory (sigma^2/n)(N-n)/(N-1) = 0.19810

§5.2 Sums of Random Variables from a Random Sample

Convolution, from first principles

If $X,Y$ are independent with densities $f_X,f_Y$, then $Z=X+Y$ has

$$\begin{aligned}F_Z(z) &= \iint_{x+y\le z} f_X(x)f_Y(y)\,dx\,dy\\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F_X(z-y)f_Y(y)\,dy,\end{aligned}$$

and differentiating under the integral (dominated convergence, since $|\partial_z F_X(z-y)| = f_X(z-y)$ is integrable against $f_Y$) gives $f_Z = f_X * f_Y$. Correct but painful; the MGF is the workhorse.

Fact (MGF of a sum of independent variables). If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are independent, $M_{\sum X_i}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t)$.

Proof.

$M_{\sum X_i}(t) = E[\prod_i e^{tX_i}] = \prod_i E[e^{tX_i}]$, the second step being the defining factorization property of independence (Fubini/Tonelli on the product measure). ∎

With the uniqueness theorem for MGFs (agreement on a neighborhood of $0$ $\Rightarrow$ equal laws), this yields reproductive properties for free:

iid componentssum $\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$
Bernoulli$(p)$Binomial$(n,p)$
Poisson$(\lambda)$Poisson$(n\lambda)$
Gamma$(\alpha,\beta)$ [Exp $=$ Gamma$(1,\beta)$]Gamma$(n\alpha,\beta)$
$N(\mu,\sigma^2)$$N(n\mu,\,n\sigma^2)$

Each is two lines: write $M_{X_i}(t)$, raise to the $n$-th power, recognize the target, invoke uniqueness.

Worked MGF examples

Poisson. If $X_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ then $M_{X_i}(t)=\exp\{\lambda(e^t-1)\}$ for all $t$, so $M_{\sum X_i}(t)=\exp\{n\lambda(e^t-1)\}$, the $\mathrm{Poisson}(n\lambda)$ MGF. Gamma. With scale parametrization, $M_{X_i}(t)=(1-\beta t)^{-\alpha}$ for $t\lt 1/\beta$, so the sum has MGF $(1-\beta t)^{-n\alpha}$, i.e. $\mathrm{Gamma}(n\alpha,\beta)$. Both MGFs are finite on a neighborhood of $0$, so uniqueness applies.

Theorem (moments of $\bar X$ and $S^2$). If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are iid with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2\lt\infty$ (no normality), then $$E[\bar X]=\mu,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad E[S^2]=\sigma^2 .$$

Proof.

The first two follow from linearity and, for the variance, independence ($\mathrm{Var}(\sum X_i)=\sum\mathrm{Var}(X_i)$). For the third use $\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum_i X_i^2-n\bar X^2$ and $E X_i^2=\sigma^2+\mu^2$, $E\bar X^2=\sigma^2/n+\mu^2$: $$E\Big[\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2\Big]=n(\sigma^2+\mu^2)-n\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\mu^2\Big)=(n-1)\sigma^2.$$ Dividing by $n-1$ gives $E[S^2]=\sigma^2$. ∎
Why $n-1$, and a caution. Deviations from $\bar X$ are systematically smaller than deviations from the unknown $\mu$ (the sample mean is the point that minimizes squared deviations), costing one degree of freedom; dividing by $n-1$ exactly repairs the bias. But $S$ itself is biased low for $\sigma$: since $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is strictly concave, Jensen gives $E[S]\lt\sqrt{E[S^2]}=\sigma$ unless $S^2$ is degenerate. Unbiasedness does not survive nonlinear transformations.
Simulation check

$n=5$ draws from $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ ($\sigma^2=1$), $200{,}000$ reps:

E[S^2]  (divisor n-1) = 0.9951   theory 1
E[SS/n] (divisor n)   = 0.7961   theory (n-1)/n = 0.8
Where it breaks: the Cauchy distribution. Everything above needs a finite mean and variance. If $X_i$ are iid standard Cauchy, the MGF does not exist, but the characteristic function does: $\varphi(t)=e^{-|t|}$. Then $\varphi_{\bar X}(t)=\varphi(t/n)^n=e^{-|t|}$, so $\bar X_n$ is again standard Cauchy for every $n$. Averaging buys nothing. This is the template for heavy-tailed data in practice (insurance losses, request latencies, trading volumes): a few extreme observations dominate the mean, and "standard error $=s/\sqrt n$" is meaningless.

§5.3 Sampling from the Normal Distribution

The chapter's centerpiece: where you earn the right to use $t$- and $F$-tables for life.

§5.3.1 Properties of $\bar X$ and $S^2$

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ iid $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i$, $S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$.

Theorem 5.3.1. (a) $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$.   (b) $\bar X \perp S^2$.   (c) $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$.

(a) is the §5.2 reproductive property scaled by $1/n$. The content is (b),(c), proven together via orthogonal transformation.

Proof of (b) and (c).

Step 1 (standardize). $Z_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$ gives $Z\in\mathbb{R}^n$ with iid $N(0,1)$ coordinates and joint density $f_Z(z)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\|z\|^2/2}$, which depends on $z$ only through $\|z\|^2$: the standard Gaussian measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is rotationally invariant. This is the engine.

Step 2 (rotate). Pick orthogonal $A$ ($AA'=I$) with first row $(\tfrac1{\sqrt n},\dots,\tfrac1{\sqrt n})$ (extend that unit vector to an ONB via Gram–Schmidt). Let $Y=AZ$. Change of variables $z=A'y$ has $|\det A'|=1$ and $\|z\|^2=\|A'y\|^2=y'AA'y=\|y\|^2$, so $f_Y(y)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\|y\|^2/2}$ — identical form. Hence $Y_1,\dots,Y_n$ are themselves iid $N(0,1)$.

Step 3 (identify pieces). $Y_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\sum Z_i=\sqrt n\,\bar Z$, and $\|Y\|^2=\|Z\|^2$, so $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{i=2}^n Y_i^2 &= \sum_i Z_i^2 - n\bar Z^2 = \sum_i(Z_i-\bar Z)^2\\ &= \sum_i\Big(\tfrac{X_i-\bar X}{\sigma}\Big)^2 = \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\end{aligned}$$ ($\mu$ cancels in the centered sum).

Step 4 (conclude).

  • $Y_2,\dots,Y_n$ iid $N(0,1)$ $\Rightarrow \sum_{i=2}^n Y_i^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ (sum of squared standard normals $=$ $\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac{n-1}{2},2)=\chi^2_{n-1}$, a §5.2 reproductive property). This is (c).
  • $Y_1$ depends only on $Y_1$; $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$ depends only on $(Y_2,\dots,Y_n)$. Mutual independence of the $Y_i$ $\Rightarrow$ functions of disjoint blocks are independent (disjoint blocks generate independent sub-σ-algebras). $\bar X$ is an affine function of $Y_1$, so $\bar X\perp S^2$. This is (b).

∎

How special this is. Independence of $\bar X$ and $S^2$ is a fingerprint of normality. Lukács (1942): for iid $X_i$ with finite variance, $\bar X\perp S^2$ only if the $X_i$ are normal. (Weaker, easy: $\mathrm{Cov}(\bar X,S^2)=\mu_3/n$, zero for any symmetric population — necessary, not sufficient.) This previews Basu's theorem (Ch. 6), which explains such independence via completeness of a sufficient statistic.
Simulation check

$n=10$, $N(5,4)$, $300{,}000$ reps:

corr(Xbar, S^2)         = -0.0009     theory: exactly 0
mean[(n-1)S^2/sigma^2]  =  8.985      theory chi2_9 mean = 9
var [(n-1)S^2/sigma^2]  = 18.035      theory chi2_9 var  = 18
var[Xbar]               =  0.4002     theory sigma^2/n   = 0.4000

Control — same experiment with iid $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ instead of normal:

corr(Xbar, S^2) = 0.6992   -- strongly nonzero

The Lukács converse, numerically: drop normality and independence vanishes outright (corr $0.70$).

§5.3.2 Student's $t$ and Snedecor's $F$

Definition (constructions). Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $W\sim\chi^2_p$ be independent. Then $$T_p := \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/p}} \;\sim\; t_p .$$ Let $U\sim\chi^2_p$ and $V\sim\chi^2_q$ be independent. Then $$F_{p,q} := \frac{U/p}{V/q} \;\sim\; F_{p,q} .$$
Why this is exactly what inference needs. For iid $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)$ and $\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$, independent. Dividing, $\sigma$ cancels: $$T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}=\frac{(\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}{\sqrt{[(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2]/(n-1)}}\sim t_{n-1}.$$ The $t$-test exists because this ratio is a pivot (its law is free of both $\mu$ and $\sigma$), built from the two independent pieces of Thm 5.3.1. Independence is what lets us define the ratio's law as "$N(0,1)$ over an independent $\sqrt{\chi^2/p}$".
Claim (the $t$ density). $f_T(t)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac{p+1}{2})}{\Gamma(p/2)\sqrt{p\pi}}\big(1+\frac{t^2}{p}\big)^{-(p+1)/2}$.

Proof.

Transform $(z,w)\mapsto(t,w)$ via $z=t\sqrt{w/p}$; Jacobian $\partial z/\partial t=\sqrt{w/p}$. Then $$\begin{aligned}f_T(t)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi p}\,2^{p/2}\Gamma(p/2)}\\ &\qquad\times\int_0^\infty w^{(p-1)/2}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac w2\big(1+\tfrac{t^2}{p}\big)\Big)dw.\end{aligned}$$ With $a=\tfrac12(1+t^2/p)$ the integral is $\Gamma(\tfrac{p+1}{2})a^{-(p+1)/2}$; collecting constants gives the result. ∎
The $F$ density follows identically ($u=\tfrac pq fv$, integrate out $v$): $$\begin{aligned}f_F(f)&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{p+q}{2})}{\Gamma(p/2)\Gamma(q/2)}\Big(\tfrac pq\Big)^{p/2}\\ &\qquad\times f^{p/2-1}\Big(1+\tfrac pq f\Big)^{-(p+q)/2}.\end{aligned}$$ Moments: $E[T_p]=0$ ($p>1$), $\mathrm{Var}(T_p)=\frac{p}{p-2}$ ($p>2$); $E[F_{p,q}]=\frac{q}{q-2}$ ($q>2$). Heavier tails than Normal/$\chi^2$ — the price of an unknown denominator (not knowing $\sigma$).

empirical 97.5th pct =  2.2720    table t_(.975,9) = 2.262

$F$ ($U\sim\chi^2_3,V\sim\chi^2_{10}$): mean $1.2497$ vs theory $q/(q-2)=1.25$. And $T_p^2\overset{d}{=}F_{1,p}$: $\mathrm{mean}(T^2)=1.2904$ vs direct $F_{1,9}$ sim $1.2840$ (both theory $1.2857$).

Supporting facts

Lemma ($\chi^2_1$ from a squared normal). If $Z\sim N(0,1)$ then $Z^2\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac12,2)=\chi^2_1$; consequently $\chi^2_p=\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac p2,2)$ is the law of a sum of $p$ independent squared standard normals.

Proof.

For $y\gt 0$, $P(Z^2\le y)=P(-\sqrt y\le Z\le\sqrt y)=2\Phi(\sqrt y)-1$. Differentiating, $f_{Z^2}(y)=\varphi(\sqrt y)\,y^{-1/2}=\dfrac{y^{-1/2}e^{-y/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$, which is the $\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac12,2)$ density since $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$. The statement for $\chi^2_p$ is the Gamma reproductive property from §5.2. ∎
Proposition (relations among $t$, $F$, Beta). (i) $T_p^2\sim F_{1,p}$.   (ii) $1/F_{p,q}\sim F_{q,p}$.   (iii) If $F\sim F_{p,q}$ then $\dfrac{(p/q)F}{1+(p/q)F}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\big(\tfrac p2,\tfrac q2\big)$.

Proof.

(i) $T_p^2=Z^2/(W/p)$ and $Z^2\sim\chi^2_1$ by the Lemma, independent of $W$. (ii) Swap the roles of numerator and denominator. (iii) Write $F=\frac{U/p}{V/q}$, so $\frac{(p/q)F}{1+(p/q)F}=\frac{U}{U+V}$ with $U\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac p2,2)$ and $V\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\tfrac q2,2)$ independent. Their joint density is proportional to $u^{p/2-1}v^{q/2-1}e^{-(u+v)/2}$. Substitute $u=sb$, $v=s(1-b)$ (Jacobian $s$): $$\begin{aligned}f_{S,B}(s,b)\;\propto\;&\underbrace{s^{(p+q)/2-1}e^{-s/2}}_{\text{in }s}\\ &\times\underbrace{b^{p/2-1}(1-b)^{q/2-1}}_{\text{in }b},\end{aligned}$$ so $B=U/(U+V)\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\tfrac p2,\tfrac q2)$, and moreover $B$ is independent of $S=U+V$. ∎
Note. The factorization in (iii) is a first glimpse of Basu's theorem (Chapter 6): $S=U+V$ is a complete sufficient statistic for the scale of a Gamma family and $U/(U+V)$ is scale-free, hence independent of it.

The $t_{n-1}$ law has heavier tails, so the nominal interval $\bar X\pm 1.96\,S/\sqrt n$ is too short. The correct multiplier is the $t_{n-1}$ quantile.

§5.4 Order Statistics

Reorder the sample ascending: $X_{(1)}\le X_{(2)}\le\dots\le X_{(n)}$. These are the raw material of the median, range, quantile estimates, extremes (max load, worst-case latency), and much of nonparametrics.

Theorem (density of a single order statistic). For iid continuous $X_i$ with cdf $F$, density $f$, the $j$-th order statistic has density $$f_{X_{(j)}}(x) = \frac{n!}{(j-1)!\,(n-j)!}\,F(x)^{\,j-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{\,n-j} f(x).$$

Proof.

Intuition: to have $X_{(j)}$ near $x$, exactly one observation sits near $x$, $j-1$ lie below, and $n-j$ lie above; the factor $\frac{n!}{(j-1)!\,1!\,(n-j)!}$ counts which observations play which role. Rigorous derivation: $X_{(j)}\le x$ happens iff at least $j$ of the $n$ independent events $\{X_i\le x\}$ occur, each with probability $p=F(x)$. Hence $$F_{X_{(j)}}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^{n}\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.$$ Differentiate in $p$ using $k\binom nk=n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ and $(n-k)\binom nk=n\binom{n-1}{k}$. With $a_k:=n\binom{n-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{n-k}$, $$\frac{d}{dp}\sum_{k=j}^{n}\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}=\sum_{k=j}^{n}\big(a_k-a_{k+1}\big)=a_j-a_{n+1}=a_j,$$ because the sum telescopes and $a_{n+1}=0$. Since $a_j=\frac{n!}{(j-1)!(n-j)!}p^{j-1}(1-p)^{n-j}$, the chain rule with $dp/dx=f(x)$ gives the stated density. ∎
The Uniform case is the Rosetta Stone. If $U_i$ iid $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$, then $U_{(j)}\sim \mathrm{Beta}(j,\,n-j+1)$ — read off the density above with $F(u)=u$. Extremes: $U_{(n)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)$ (max), $U_{(1)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,n)$ (min). And via the probability integral transform $U_i=F(X_i)$, every continuous case reduces to the uniform one — so Beta order statistics underlie everything.

Extremes: max mean $0.9089$ vs $n/(n+1)=0.9091$; min mean $0.0911$ vs $1/(n+1)=0.0909$.

Joint laws. (a) The vector of all order statistics has density $n!\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_{(i)})$ on $\{x_{(1)}\lt\dots\lt x_{(n)}\}$. (b) For $i\lt j$, the pair $(X_{(i)},X_{(j)})$ has density $$\begin{aligned}f(u,v)&=\frac{n!}{(i-1)!\,(j-i-1)!\,(n-j)!}\\ &\quad\times f(u)f(v)\,F(u)^{i-1}\big[F(v)-F(u)\big]^{j-i-1}\\ &\quad\times\big[1-F(v)\big]^{n-j},\qquad u\lt v.\end{aligned}$$

Proof of (a).

Continuity makes ties null events. The $n!$ orderings of the labels are equally likely, and each maps its region of $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto the ordered cone with density $\prod f(x_i)$. Summing the $n!$ identical contributions gives the stated density. Part (b) follows by integrating out the other coordinates, or by the same "who sits where" counting as the single-order-statistic case. ∎
Example: the range. For $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ data take $i=1,\ j=n$: $f(u,v)=n(n-1)(v-u)^{n-2}$ on $0\lt u\lt v\lt 1$. With $R=V-U$, integrating over $u\in(0,1-r)$ gives $f_R(r)=n(n-1)r^{n-2}(1-r)$, i.e. $R\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n-1,2)$ with $E[R]=\frac{n-1}{n+1}$. The sample median of an odd sample $n=2m+1$ is $U_{(m+1)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(m+1,m+1)$.
Simulation check

$200{,}000$ reps each, $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ data:

Real-world: extremes and "the tail at scale". $P(X_{(n)}\le x)=F(x)^n$. If a page load fans out to $100$ backend calls and each is independently slower than its own 99th percentile with probability $0.01$, then the probability that at least one exceeds that threshold is $1-0.99^{100}\approx 0.634$. Typical user experience is governed by the maximum of many latencies, not by any single one, which is why engineers track high percentiles rather than means.

§5.5 Convergence Concepts

This section is where measure-theoretic probability earns its keep. Four modes, a strict hierarchy, and the delta method — the asymptotic toolkit all of Chapter 10 runs on.

§5.5.1 Convergence in Probability

$X_n\xrightarrow{P}X$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$, $P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)\to 0$. A statement about the measure of a bad set shrinking, for each tolerance separately.
Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN). If $X_i$ iid with $E[X_1]=\mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2\lt\infty$, then $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu$.

Proof.

$E[\bar X_n]=\mu$, $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$. Chebyshev: $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon)\le \frac{\sigma^2/n}{\varepsilon^2}\to 0.$ ∎
(The finite-variance assumption is removable — the WLLN holds under just $E|X_1|\lt\infty$ — but Chebyshev is the honest one-liner.)
Simulation check

$X_i$ iid $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$, $\mu=1$. Empirical $P(|\bar X_n-1|>0.1)$:

 n=   10:  0.7507
 n=   50:  0.4795
 n=  200:  0.1578
 n= 1000:  0.0019
 n= 5000:  0.0000

The bad-set probability marches to $0$, exactly as $\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)=1/(0.01 n)$ predicts as an upper bound.

§5.5.2 Almost Sure Convergence

$X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X$ if $P(\{\omega: X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)\})=1$ — convergence of the sample paths for almost every $\omega$. Strictly stronger than $\xrightarrow{P}$: a.s. $\Rightarrow$ in probability, not conversely.
Strong Law (SLLN), stated. If $X_i$ iid with $E|X_1|\lt\infty$, then $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu$. (Proof deferred — it needs Kolmogorov's maximal inequality or a truncation argument; not a one-liner.)
Flag — what is and is not proved here. Two results on this page are stated without proof. (i) The SLLN under the sharp assumption $E|X_1|\lt\infty$: the Borel–Cantelli argument below proves it only under the much stronger $EX_1^4\lt\infty$, and closing that gap is real work (Etemadi's truncation proof, or Kolmogorov's inequality plus a subsequence argument). (ii) The portmanteau lemma in §5.5.3, which is quoted in order to make the continuous mapping theorem a one-liner. Both are standard and neither is in doubt; they are simply not established here. Named results cited in passing on this page (Lukács 1942 for the $\bar X\perp S^2$ converse, de Finetti, Lévy continuity, Smirnov/probability integral transform) are attributions given from memory and not checked against the original papers; the statements are standard, but verify the citation before quoting one.
Borel–Cantelli criterion, and the SLLN under a fourth moment. (a) If $\sum_n P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon)\lt\infty$ for every $\varepsilon\gt0$, then $X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X$. (b) If additionally $E X_1^4\lt\infty$, then $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu$.

Proof.

(a) By the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, $P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon\text{ i.o.})=0$ for each $\varepsilon$. Intersecting over $\varepsilon=1/k$, $k\in\mathbb N$, gives $P(X_n\to X)=1$. (b) Let $Y_i=X_i-\mu$ with $E Y_i^2=\sigma^2$, $E Y_i^4=\mu_4$. Expanding $E(\sum Y_i)^4$, any term in which some index appears exactly once has mean zero. What survives is $n$ terms $E Y_i^4$ and $3n(n-1)$ terms of the form $E Y_i^2E Y_j^2$, so $$E(\bar X_n-\mu)^4=\frac{n\mu_4+3n(n-1)\sigma^4}{n^4}\le\frac{\mu_4+3\sigma^4}{n^2}.$$ Markov's inequality gives $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\gt\varepsilon)\le\dfrac{\mu_4+3\sigma^4}{\varepsilon^4 n^2}$, which is summable in $n$. Apply (a). ∎
(The typewriter sequence below has $\sum_n P(A_n)=\sum_m m\cdot\frac1m=\infty$, consistent with (a) not applying.)
Why the distinction is not pedantic. "In probability" says that at any fixed large $n$, you are probably close. "Almost surely" says that if you watch a single run forever, it settles. A long-running estimator, an online learning algorithm, or a Monte Carlo average you keep refining — those are a.s. statements: you care about the one trajectory you are on, not a snapshot.
Simulation check — the typewriter sequence (in probability but NOT a.s.)

On $([0,1],\text{Lebesgue})$, index by sweeps: $n=\tfrac{m(m-1)}2+k$, $A_{m,k}=[\tfrac{k-1}{m},\tfrac km)$, $X_n=\mathbf 1_{A_{m,k}}$. Then $P(A_n)=1/m\to 0$ so $X_n\xrightarrow{P}0$; but every fixed $\omega$ is hit once per sweep, so $X_n(\omega)=1$ infinitely often — no pointwise convergence. Tracking $\omega=0.37$:

sweep m= 1:  n=  1   X_n=1   P(A_n)=1.0000
sweep m= 3:  n=  5   X_n=1   P(A_n)=0.3333
sweep m= 5:  n= 12   X_n=1   P(A_n)=0.2000
sweep m= 7:  n= 24   X_n=1   P(A_n)=0.1429

$X_n(0.37)$ keeps spiking to $1$ forever even as $P(A_n)\to 0$. This is the canonical separation of the two modes.

§5.5.3 Convergence in Distribution

$X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$ if $F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)$ at every continuity point $x$ of $F_X$. The weakest mode — only the laws converge, the variables need not live on a common space. (a.s. $\Rightarrow$ $P$ $\Rightarrow$ $d$; none of the arrows reverse in general.)
Lemma (portmanteau, the part we need). $X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$ if and only if $E\big[f(X_n)\big]\to E\big[f(X)\big]$ for every bounded continuous $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$.
Not proved here. This is the standard characterization of weak convergence (Billingsley, Convergence of Probability Measures); the forward direction is an integration-by-parts/approximation argument and the reverse direction follows by approximating indicators of $(-\infty,x]$ at continuity points. It is quoted because it makes the next theorem a one-liner.
Continuous Mapping Theorem. Let $g$ be continuous (on a set containing the range of $X$ with probability $1$). Then $$X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\Rightarrow g(X_n)\xrightarrow{a.s.}g(X),$$ $$X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\Rightarrow g(X_n)\xrightarrow{P}g(X),$$ $$X_n\xrightarrow{d}X\Rightarrow g(X_n)\xrightarrow{d}g(X).$$

Proof.

Almost sure. On the probability-one event where $X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)$ and $g$ is continuous at $X(\omega)$, continuity gives $g(X_n(\omega))\to g(X(\omega))$ directly. In probability. Fix $\varepsilon,\eta\gt0$. Choose $M$ with $P(|X|\gt M)\lt\eta/2$ (possible since $X$ is a.s. finite). On the compact set $[-M-1,M+1]$ the function $g$ is uniformly continuous, so there is $\delta\in(0,1)$ such that $|g(u)-g(v)|\le\varepsilon$ whenever $u,v\in[-M-1,M+1]$ and $|u-v|\le\delta$. If $|g(X_n)-g(X)|\gt\varepsilon$ then either $|X|\gt M$ or $|X_n-X|\gt\delta$, so $$P\big(|g(X_n)-g(X)|\gt\varepsilon\big)\le P(|X|\gt M)+P(|X_n-X|\gt\delta).$$ The second term $\to0$ by assumption, so the $\limsup$ is at most $\eta/2$; letting $\eta\downarrow0$ gives the claim. In distribution. Let $f$ be bounded and continuous. Then $f\circ g$ is bounded and continuous, so by the Lemma applied to $X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$, $$E\big[f(g(X_n))\big]\to E\big[f(g(X))\big].$$ Since this holds for every bounded continuous $f$, the Lemma read in the reverse direction gives $g(X_n)\xrightarrow{d}g(X)$. ∎
Why this theorem earns its keep. It is the workhorse that lets you move limits through functions without redoing any analysis, and it is used constantly later: $S\xrightarrow{P}\sigma$ from $S^2\xrightarrow{P}\sigma^2$ via $g=\sqrt\cdot$ (needed for Slutsky and the $t$-statistic below), the consistency of method-of-moments estimators (§7.2.1, where $\hat\theta=g(m_1,\dots,m_k)$ for continuous $g$), the squared limit in the second-order delta method (§5.5.4), and the $\chi^2$ limits of likelihood ratio statistics (§10.3.1). The multivariate statement is identical with $g:\mathbb R^k\to\mathbb R^m$ continuous, and that is the version §7.2.1 actually needs.
Central Limit Theorem. If $X_i$ iid, $E[X_1]=\mu$, $0\lt\mathrm{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2\lt\infty$, then $\dfrac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)$.

Proof sketch (characteristic functions).

Let $Y_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$, $\varphi(t)=E[e^{itY_1}]=1-\tfrac{t^2}2+o(t^2)$. The standardized sum has characteristic function $\varphi(t/\sqrt n)^n=\big(1-\tfrac{t^2}{2n}+o(1/n)\big)^n\to e^{-t^2/2}$, the $N(0,1)$ characteristic function. Lévy's continuity theorem converts pointwise convergence of characteristic functions into $\xrightarrow{d}$. ∎
Simulation check — CLT kicking in

Standardized $\bar X_n$ from iid $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ (a skewed start). Tail probabilities vs $N(0,1)$ (target $\Phi(-1)=0.1587$ both sides):

n=  2:  P(Z<-1)=0.1176   P(Z>1)=0.1452   (visibly skewed)
n= 10:  P(Z<-1)=0.1539   P(Z>1)=0.1542
n= 50:  P(Z<-1)=0.1582   P(Z>1)=0.1580
n=200:  P(Z<-1)=0.1573   P(Z>1)=0.1581

The left/right asymmetry at $n=2$ (the exponential's skew) is symmetrized away by $n=50$ — the CLT visibly repairing a skewed summand.

The hierarchy, proved. (i) $X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\Rightarrow X_n\xrightarrow{P}X$. (ii) $X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\Rightarrow X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$. (iii) If $X_n\xrightarrow{d}c$ for a constant $c$, then $X_n\xrightarrow{P}c$.

Proof.

(i) Fix $\varepsilon$ and let $A_n=\bigcup_{m\ge n}\{|X_m-X|\gt\varepsilon\}$, a decreasing sequence. $\bigcap_n A_n\subset\{X_m\not\to X\}$, a null set, so by continuity from above $P(A_n)\downarrow0$, and $P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon)\le P(A_n)\to0$. (ii) Let $x$ be a continuity point of $F_X$ and $\varepsilon\gt0$. Then $\{X_n\le x\}\subset\{X\le x+\varepsilon\}\cup\{|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon\}$, so $\limsup F_{X_n}(x)\le F_X(x+\varepsilon)$. Symmetrically $\liminf F_{X_n}(x)\ge F_X(x-\varepsilon)$. Let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and use continuity at $x$. (iii) The cdf of the constant $c$ is $\mathbf 1\{x\ge c\}$, continuous except at $c$. So $P(|X_n-c|\gt\varepsilon)\le F_{X_n}(c-\varepsilon)+1-F_{X_n}(c+\varepsilon)\to0+1-1=0$. ∎
Slutsky's theorem. If $X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$ and $Y_n\xrightarrow{P}c$ (a constant), then $X_n+Y_n\xrightarrow{d}X+c$, $\ X_nY_n\xrightarrow{d}cX$, and $X_n/Y_n\xrightarrow{d}X/c$ for $c\ne0$.

Proof sketch.

Since $Y_n-c\xrightarrow{P}0$, one shows $(X_n,Y_n)\xrightarrow{d}(X,c)$ (for example, via characteristic functions and $|E e^{i(sX_n+tY_n)}-E e^{i(sX_n+tc)}|\le E|e^{it(Y_n-c)}-1|\to0$). The three conclusions then follow from the continuous mapping theorem applied to $(x,y)\mapsto x+y,\ xy,\ x/y$. ∎
Why you care: the $t$-statistic without normality. For iid data with finite variance (any shape), $S\xrightarrow{P}\sigma$ by the WLLN and continuous mapping. Slutsky then gives $$\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma}{S/\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}\frac{N(0,1)}{1}.$$ So $t$-based intervals are asymptotically valid far outside the normal model. The word "asymptotically" is doing real work, though: the CLT approximation is only as good as the skewness of the data allows.
Simulation check

Coverage of the nominal 95% $t$ interval for the mean of $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ data (true mean $1$), $50{,}000$ reps per $n$:

n=  10: coverage = 0.9045
n=  30: coverage = 0.9277
n= 100: coverage = 0.9431

Coverage climbs toward $0.95$ as Slutsky/CLT predict, but even at $n=30$ it is $92.8\%$. The "$n\ge30$ is enough" rule of thumb fails for skewed outcomes such as revenue per user or time-to-event data.

§5.5.4 The Delta Method

Delta method. If $\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $g$ is differentiable at $\theta$ with $g'(\theta)\ne 0$, then $$\sqrt n\big(g(T_n)-g(\theta)\big)\xrightarrow{d}N\!\big(0,\;\sigma^2\,[g'(\theta)]^2\big).$$

Proof.

Taylor: $g(T_n)=g(\theta)+g'(\theta)(T_n-\theta)+R_n$ with $R_n=o(|T_n-\theta|)$. Since $T_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta$ (consistency from the $\sqrt n$-rate), $\sqrt n R_n\xrightarrow{P}0$. Thus $\sqrt n(g(T_n)-g(\theta))=g'(\theta)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)+o_P(1)$, and Slutsky gives the limit $N(0,\sigma^2[g'(\theta)]^2)$. ∎
Why you use this constantly. It is how you get a standard error for any smooth transform of an asymptotically normal estimate — log-odds, ratios, $R^2$, a rate computed from a count — without re-deriving a sampling distribution. It is the mechanical engine behind "SE of a transformed parameter" in every stats package.
Simulation check

$X_i$ iid $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.3)$, $g=\mathrm{logit}$, $g'(p)=\frac1{p(1-p)}=4.762$. Theory: $\sqrt n(\mathrm{logit}\,\bar X-\mathrm{logit}\,p)\to N(0,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2=p(1-p)g'(p)^2=\frac1{p(1-p)}=4.762$.

 n=   50: empirical var = 5.1500   theory = 4.7619
 n=  500: empirical var = 4.7890   theory = 4.7619
 n= 5000: empirical var = 4.7595   theory = 4.7619

Convergence to the delta-method variance is clear; the $n=50$ overshoot is the finite-sample curvature the first-order expansion ignores.

Second-order delta method. If $\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^2)$, $g'(\theta)=0$ and $g''(\theta)\ne0$ exists, then $$n\big(g(T_n)-g(\theta)\big)\xrightarrow{d}\frac{\sigma^2 g''(\theta)}{2}\,\chi^2_1 .$$

Proof.

Taylor with the vanishing first derivative: $g(T_n)=g(\theta)+\tfrac12g''(\theta)(T_n-\theta)^2+o_P(n^{-1})$. Multiply by $n$: $n(g(T_n)-g(\theta))=\tfrac12g''(\theta)\big[\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\big]^2+o_P(1)$. The bracket converges in law to $\sigma Z$ with $Z\sim N(0,1)$; apply the continuous mapping theorem and Slutsky. ∎
Why it matters. The first-order delta method silently breaks where $g'(\theta)=0$: it predicts a degenerate limit ($\sigma^2[g'(\theta)]^2=0$) while the true fluctuations are of order $1/n$, not $1/\sqrt n$, and are non-normal and one-sided. Examples: $g(p)=p(1-p)$ at $p=\tfrac12$ (a variance estimate for a fair coin), or any smooth criterion at an interior optimum. Recognize this failure mode before trusting a standard error.
Simulation check

n= 400: mean = -0.2494   var = 0.1248   P(-4V <= 3.841) = 0.9491   (chi2_1: 0.9500)

(Here $-4n(g(\bar X)-\tfrac14)=\big[\sqrt n(2\bar X-1)\big]^2$ exactly, a squared asymptotically normal variable, so the $\chi^2_1$ limit is the CLT squared.)

§5.6 Generating a Random Sample

Every simulation above rests on turning uniform random numbers into draws from a target law. Two families: direct (exploit structure) and indirect (accept/reject).

§5.6.1 Direct Methods — the Probability Integral Transform

Inverse-CDF (Smirnov) transform. If $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $F$ is a cdf with generalized inverse $F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge u\}$, then $X=F^{-1}(U)$ has cdf $F$.

Proof.

For continuous strictly increasing $F$: $P(F^{-1}(U)\le x)=P(U\le F(x))=F(x)$, the middle step by monotonicity of $F$. The generalized inverse handles jumps/flats so the identity $\{F^{-1}(u)\le x\}=\{u\le F(x)\}$ holds in general. ∎
Practical reach: exponential ($X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$), and any distribution with a closed-form quantile. Normal has no elementary $F^{-1}$ — hence Box–Muller (a direct method exploiting the 2-D rotational structure of §5.3) or accept/reject.
Simulation check

$\mathrm{Exp}(\text{rate}=2)$ via $X=-\ln(1-U)/2$, $300{,}000$ draws:

empirical mean = 0.5003   theory 1/lam   = 0.5000
empirical var  = 0.2499   theory 1/lam^2 = 0.2500

§5.6.2 Indirect Methods

When $F^{-1}$ has no usable closed form, exploit structural relationships between distributions to build the draw from simpler ones. Everything in §5.2–5.3 is a recipe of this kind: $\chi^2_p$ is a sum of $p$ squared normals, $t_p$ and $F_{p,q}$ are ratios of independent such pieces, $\mathrm{Beta}$ is a ratio of Gammas, and $\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$ is a sum of Bernoullis. Two further workhorses:

Box–Muller. Let $U_1,U_2$ be iid $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and set $R=\sqrt{-2\ln U_1}$, $\Theta=2\pi U_2$. Then $Z_1=R\cos\Theta$ and $Z_2=R\sin\Theta$ are iid $N(0,1)$.

Proof.

Run the map backwards. If $(Z_1,Z_2)$ are iid $N(0,1)$, then in polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ the density is $\frac1{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}\cdot r$ (Jacobian $r$). This factors as $\Theta\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,2\pi)$ independent of $R$ with density $re^{-r^2/2}$. For that $R$, $P(R^2\gt s)=\int_{\sqrt s}^\infty re^{-r^2/2}dr=e^{-s/2}$, so $R^2$ is exponential with mean $2$. And $-2\ln U_1$ has exactly this law: $P(-2\ln U_1\gt s)=P(U_1\lt e^{-s/2})=e^{-s/2}$. The two constructions have the same joint law of $(R,\Theta)$, hence of $(Z_1,Z_2)$. ∎
Poisson from exponentials. Let $E_1,E_2,\dots$ be iid $\mathrm{Exp}(\text{rate}=\lambda)$, $S_k=E_1+\dots+E_k$, and $N=\max\{k:S_k\le1\}$ (with $N=0$ if $E_1\gt1$). Then $N\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$.

Proof.

$\{N\ge k\}=\{S_k\le1\}$ and $S_k\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(k,1/\lambda)$. Repeated integration by parts of the Gamma density gives the classical identity $P(S_k\le1)=\int_0^{\lambda}\frac{s^{k-1}e^{-s}}{(k-1)!}ds=P(\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)\ge k)$ for every $k\ge1$. Two integer-valued variables with identical upper tails have the same pmf. ∎
Why it matters. The Poisson construction is the statement "event counts are what you see when waiting times are memoryless". It is the basis for simulating arrivals in queueing and traffic models. Box–Muller is the prototype of a direct sampler for a law with no elementary quantile function.

§5.6.3 The Accept/Reject Algorithm

Accept/Reject. To sample target density $f$ using proposal density $g$ (easy to sample) with $M=\sup_x f(x)/g(x)\lt\infty$: draw $Y\sim g$, $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ independent; accept $X=Y$ if $U\le \frac{f(Y)}{M\,g(Y)}$, else repeat.

Proof of correctness.

$P(\text{accept}\mid Y=y)=\frac{f(y)}{Mg(y)}$, so for any Borel $B$, $$P(Y\in B,\text{accept})=\int_B \frac{f(y)}{Mg(y)}g(y)\,dy=\frac1M\int_B f(y)\,dy.$$ Taking $B=\mathbb{R}$: $P(\text{accept})=1/M$. Hence $P(X\in B)=P(Y\in B\mid\text{accept})=\int_B f=\;$ target. Each trial accepts independently w.p. $1/M$, so the number of trials is $\mathrm{Geometric}(1/M)$ with mean $M$. ∎
The intuition and the cost. Throw darts under the scaled proposal $M g$; keep those landing under $f$. $M$ is the "wasted area" factor — the mean number of proposals per accepted draw. Choose $g$ hugging $f$ to keep $M$ near $1$. This is the conceptual ancestor of MCMC: when $M$ is uncontrollable (high dimensions), you give up independence and build a Markov chain with $f$ as its stationary law instead.
Simulation check

Target $\mathrm{Beta}(2,3)$, proposal $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$, $M=\sup f=f(1/3)=1.7778$. Collected $400{,}000$ accepted draws:

proposed 710,717 candidates for 400,000 accepts
empirical acceptance = 0.5628   theory 1/M = 0.5625
empirical mean       = 0.3998   theory a/(a+b) = 0.4000

  0.32   |     1.7652        |    1.7769
  0.38   |     1.7476        |    1.7578
  0.47   |     1.5668        |    1.5711

Acceptance rate matches $1/M$ to three decimals, and the kept draws reproduce the Beta density across its support.

Two refinements. (1) $M\ge1$ always, since $\int f=\int g=1$ forces $\sup f/g\ge1$; $M=1$ only if $f=g$ a.e. (2) The target need only be known up to a constant: if $f=\tilde f/c$ and $\tilde M=\sup\tilde f/g$, accepting when $U\le\tilde f(Y)/(\tilde Mg(Y))$ still returns exact draws from $f$, with acceptance probability $c/\tilde M$. That is exactly the situation in Bayesian posteriors (prior $\times$ likelihood without the normalizing integral), and it is the doorway to MCMC.

Monte Carlo error: a simulation is itself a statistic

If $\theta=E[h(X)]$ is estimated by $\hat\theta_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N h(X_i)$ with $X_i$ drawn iid, the WLLN gives $\hat\theta_N\xrightarrow{P}\theta$ and the CLT gives $\sqrt N(\hat\theta_N-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\mathrm{Var}\,h(X))$. The standard error shrinks like $1/\sqrt N$: each extra decimal digit costs $100\times$ more draws. This is also how to report the uncertainty of every simulation check on this page.

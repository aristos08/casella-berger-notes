Chapter 10 — Asymptotic Evaluations Why maximum likelihood wins in the limit, and how badly the limit can mislead

Every chapter so far has deferred something to this one. Chapter 7 said the MLE's justification is asymptotic; Chapter 8 said $-2\log\lambda$ has a $\chi^2$ limit; Chapter 9 found a "95%" interval with minimum coverage $0.0198$ and left it unexplained. All three debts are settled here. The chapter also has a second, equally important theme: asymptotic optimality is a statement about a limit, and the rate at which the limit is approached is a separate question that the theory does not answer.

§10.1 Point Estimation

§10.1.1 Consistency

Definition. A sequence of estimators $W_n$ is consistent for $\theta$ if $W_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta$ for every $\theta\in\Theta$. It is strongly consistent if the convergence is almost sure.
Proposition (the easy sufficient condition). If $\mathrm{Bias}_\theta(W_n)\to0$ and $\mathrm{Var}_\theta(W_n)\to0$ for every $\theta$, then $W_n$ is consistent.

Proof.

By the bias–variance decomposition (§7.3.1), $\mathrm{MSE}(\theta,W_n)=\mathrm{Var}_\theta(W_n)+\mathrm{Bias}_\theta(W_n)^2\to0$, and Chebyshev gives $$P_\theta\big(|W_n-\theta|\gt\varepsilon\big)\le\frac{\mathrm{MSE}(\theta,W_n)}{\varepsilon^2}\to0 . \qquad\blacksquare$$
Consistency is therefore cheap: the WLLN makes $\bar X_n$ consistent for $\mu$, continuous mapping (§5.5.3) extends that to any continuous function of moments, and §7.2.1 used exactly this to get consistency of method-of-moments estimators.
Theorem (consistency of the MLE — the argument, with its gap marked). Let $X_i$ be iid $f(x\mid\theta_0)$, and suppose the model is identifiable. The key inequality is that the expected log-likelihood is uniquely maximized at the truth: for $\theta\ne\theta_0$, $$E_{\theta_0}\big[\log f(X\mid\theta)\big]\;\lt\;E_{\theta_0}\big[\log f(X\mid\theta_0)\big].$$

Proof of the inequality.

The difference is a Kullback–Leibler divergence. By Jensen's inequality applied to the strictly concave $\log$, $$E_{\theta_0}\left[\log\frac{f(X\mid\theta)}{f(X\mid\theta_0)}\right]\le\log E_{\theta_0}\left[\frac{f(X\mid\theta)}{f(X\mid\theta_0)}\right]=\log\int f(x\mid\theta)\,dx=\log 1=0,$$ with equality iff the ratio is constant a.s., i.e. iff $f(\cdot\mid\theta)=f(\cdot\mid\theta_0)$ a.e., which identifiability excludes. ∎

Now the heuristic that this makes rigorous: by the WLLN, for each fixed $\theta$ $$\frac1n\ell_n(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i\log f(X_i\mid\theta)\;\xrightarrow{P}\;E_{\theta_0}\big[\log f(X\mid\theta)\big],$$ a function maximized uniquely at $\theta_0$. So the random objective converges pointwise to a limit whose maximizer is the truth, and one expects $\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta_0$.

Flag — pointwise convergence does not by itself give consistency. The step from "$\frac1n\ell_n(\theta)\to$ limit for each fixed $\theta$" to "the argmax converges" is not automatic, and the gap is real: a sequence of functions can converge pointwise while their maximizers run off elsewhere. Closing it needs a uniform law of large numbers over $\theta$ (plus compactness, or a condition keeping the likelihood from escaping to the boundary) — Wald's 1949 conditions, or the modern empirical-process treatment. I have proved the information inequality that makes the argument work and stated the rest honestly: this page does not prove MLE consistency. Note also that it genuinely fails in some models — the normal-mixture likelihood of §7.2.4 is unbounded, so there is no MLE at all to be consistent.

§10.1.2 Efficiency

Definition. $W_n$ is asymptotically efficient for $\theta$ if $$\sqrt n\,(W_n-\theta)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\!\left(0,\;\frac{1}{I_1(\theta)}\right),$$ where $I_1$ is the Fisher information in one observation (§7.3.2). The limiting variance is the Cramér–Rao bound, so no estimator can do asymptotically better in this sense.
Theorem (asymptotic normality and efficiency of the MLE). Under regularity conditions — $\hat\theta_n$ consistent, $\theta_0$ interior to $\Theta$, $\log f$ three-times differentiable with the information finite and the usual interchange of derivative and integral valid — $$\sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\!\left(0,\tfrac{1}{I_1(\theta_0)}\right).$$

Proof.

Since $\theta_0$ is interior and $\hat\theta_n$ maximizes $\ell_n$, eventually $\ell_n'(\hat\theta_n)=0$. Expand the score about $\theta_0$ with a mean-value remainder: for some $\tilde\theta_n$ between $\hat\theta_n$ and $\theta_0$, $$0=\ell_n'(\hat\theta_n)=\ell_n'(\theta_0)+(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)\,\ell_n''(\tilde\theta_n).$$ Solving and scaling by $\sqrt n$, $$\sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)=\frac{\ell_n'(\theta_0)/\sqrt n}{-\ell_n''(\tilde\theta_n)/n}.$$ Numerator. $\ell_n'(\theta_0)=\sum_iS_i$ where $S_i=\partial_\theta\log f(X_i\mid\theta_0)$ are iid with $E[S_i]=0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(S_i)=I_1(\theta_0)$ (both established in §7.3.2). The CLT gives $$\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_iS_i\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\big(0,I_1(\theta_0)\big).$$ Denominator. $-\ell_n''(\theta_0)/n$ is an average of iid terms $-\partial^2_\theta\log f(X_i\mid\theta_0)$ with mean $I_1(\theta_0)$ (the second information identity), so by the WLLN it converges in probability to $I_1(\theta_0)$. Since $\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta_0$ forces $\tilde\theta_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta_0$, the regularity conditions let us replace $\tilde\theta_n$ by $\theta_0$ with an $o_P(1)$ error, so the denominator also $\xrightarrow{P}I_1(\theta_0)$. Combine. By Slutsky (§5.5.3), $$\sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)\xrightarrow{d}\frac{N(0,I_1(\theta_0))}{I_1(\theta_0)}=N\!\left(0,\tfrac{1}{I_1(\theta_0)}\right). \qquad\blacksquare$$
What the proof reveals. Every tool from Chapter 5 appears exactly once: CLT on the score, WLLN on the curvature, Slutsky to divide, continuous mapping inside the regularity step. And the reason the MLE is efficient is now visible — the asymptotic variance is $\mathrm{Var}(\text{score})/\big(E[\text{curvature}]\big)^2$, and the two information identities make numerator and denominator the same quantity $I_1$, so one factor cancels. For any other estimating equation those two quantities differ, and the ratio is strictly larger: that is exactly the M-estimator variance formula in §10.2.2.
Invariance, via the delta method. Combining with §5.5.4: if $\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1/I_1(\theta))$ and $\tau$ is differentiable with $\tau'(\theta)\ne0$, then $$\sqrt n\big(\tau(\hat\theta_n)-\tau(\theta)\big)\xrightarrow{d}N\!\left(0,\frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{I_1(\theta)}\right),$$ and the limiting variance is precisely the Cramér–Rao bound for $\tau(\theta)$. So the MLE of any smooth function of $\theta$ is asymptotically efficient for it — which, combined with the exact invariance of §7.2.2, is the real reason maximum likelihood is the default.

§10.1.3 Calculations and Comparisons

Definition (asymptotic relative efficiency). If $\sqrt n(W_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma_W^2)$ and $\sqrt n(V_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma_V^2)$, the ARE of $W$ relative to $V$ is $\sigma_V^2/\sigma_W^2$. An ARE of $0.5$ means $W$ needs twice the sample size to match $V$.
Theorem (asymptotic law of the sample median). Let $X_i$ be iid with cdf $F$ and a density $f$ continuous and positive at the median $m$. Then $$\sqrt n\,(\hat m_n-m)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\!\left(0,\;\frac{1}{4f(m)^2}\right).$$

Proof sketch.

Fix $t$ and let $p_n=P\big(\sqrt n(\hat m_n-m)\le t\big)=P\big(\hat m_n\le m+t/\sqrt n\big)$. The median is $\le c$ exactly when at least half the observations are $\le c$, so with $B_n=\#\{i:X_i\le m+t/\sqrt n\}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$ and $p=F(m+t/\sqrt n)$, $$p_n=P\big(B_n\ge n/2\big).$$ A Taylor expansion gives $p=\tfrac12+f(m)t/\sqrt n+o(n^{-1/2})$, so $B_n$ has mean $n/2+\sqrt n f(m)t+o(\sqrt n)$ and variance $n/4+o(n)$. Standardizing $B_n$ and applying the CLT, $$P(B_n\ge n/2)\to\Phi\big(2f(m)\,t\big),$$ which is the cdf of $N\big(0,1/(4f(m)^2)\big)$ at $t$. ∎
(The step treating $o(\cdot)$ terms as negligible inside the CLT is where a fully rigorous proof needs more care; the standard route is via the order-statistic representation of §5.4.)
The comparison, and why it flips. The mean has asymptotic variance $\sigma^2$; the median has $1/(4f(m)^2)$. So everything depends on how much mass sits near the centre versus in the tails:
  • Normal$(\mu,\sigma^2)$. $f(\mu)=1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, so the median's variance is $\pi\sigma^2/2$ and $\mathrm{ARE}(\text{median},\text{mean})=2/\pi\approx0.6366$: the median wastes $36\%$ of the data.
  • Laplace$(\mu,b)$. Here $\mathrm{Var}=2b^2$ while $f(\mu)=1/(2b)$ gives median variance $b^2$. The ARE is $2$: the median is twice as efficient, and indeed it is the MLE for this model.
Neither estimator dominates — the answer is a property of the tails, not of the estimators.

§10.1.4 Bootstrap Standard Errors

The idea. We want the sampling sd of $\hat\theta=T(X_1,\dots,X_n)$, which depends on the unknown $F$. The bootstrap substitutes the empirical cdf $\hat F_n$ for $F$ and computes the resulting sd — the plug-in principle. In practice: draw $B$ resamples of size $n$ with replacement from the observed data, recompute $T$ on each, and take the sample sd of those $B$ values.
Two approximations, only one of which $B$ controls. The bootstrap SE involves (i) replacing $F$ by $\hat F_n$ and (ii) approximating the resulting resampling expectation by $B$ Monte Carlo draws. Taking $B$ large removes (ii) entirely — it is just computation — but does nothing about (i), which is the statistical approximation and the one that can fail. Conflating them is the most common bootstrap misconception.
Simulation check — it works, with no formula required

$n=25$ from $N(0,1)$, $B=400$ resamples, $400$ outer replications:

  mean  : mean bootstrap SE = 0.1955   actual sd of statistic = 0.2032   theory 0.2000
  median: mean bootstrap SE = 0.2574   actual sd of statistic = 0.2504   theory   --

For the mean there is a formula to check against; for the median there is not (the delta method would need $f$ at the median, as in §10.1.3). The bootstrap delivers a usable SE for both without knowing either.

Where the bootstrap fails: extremes. Let $X_i$ be iid $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,\theta)$, so $X_{(n)}$ is the MLE. The probability that a single bootstrap resample even contains the observed maximum is $$1-\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)^n\;\longrightarrow\;1-e^{-1}\approx0.6321 .$$ Hence the bootstrap distribution of $X^*_{(n)}$ puts an atom of mass $\approx0.632$ on the observed $X_{(n)}$, i.e. the bootstrapped pivot has a point mass at $0$. But the true limit is continuous: $n\big(\theta-X_{(n)}\big)/\theta\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Exponential}(1)$. So the bootstrap approximates a continuous law by one with a $0.63$ atom — it converges to the wrong thing, and no increase in $B$ or $n$ repairs it.

Proof of the limit law.

$P\big(n(\theta-X_{(n)})/\theta\gt t\big)=P\big(X_{(n)}\lt\theta(1-t/n)\big)=(1-t/n)^n\to e^{-t}$ for $t\in(0,n)$, using $P(X_{(n)}\le x)=(x/\theta)^n$ from §5.1. ∎
Simulation check — the spurious atom
n=50: P(a bootstrap resample contains the sample max) = 0.6347
      exact 1-(1-1/n)^n = 0.6358      limit 1-1/e = 0.6321

true pivot      n(theta - X_(n)):  P(=0) = 0.0000   median = 0.6909
                                   (Exponential(1) median = log 2 = 0.6931)
bootstrap pivot n(X_(n) - X*_(n)): P(=0) = 0.6328   <- a point mass that should not exist

The true pivot's simulated median matches $\log 2$ to three decimals, confirming the Exponential$(1)$ limit; the bootstrap version is $63\%$ concentrated at a single point. The general lesson: the bootstrap needs the functional to be smooth in $F$, and maxima, minima and boundary parameters are not. Remedies exist (the $m$-out-of-$n$ bootstrap, subsampling) but they change the procedure.

§10.2 Robustness

§10.2.1 The Mean and the Median

Definition (breakdown point). The breakdown point of an estimator is the smallest fraction of the sample that, if replaced by arbitrary values, can drive the estimate to an arbitrary value. The mean has breakdown point $0$ (one observation sent to $\infty$ drags $\bar X$ with it); the median has $1/2$; the $\alpha$-trimmed mean has $\alpha$.
Efficiency and robustness are different axes. §10.1.3 compared estimators within a correctly specified model. Robustness asks what happens when the model is slightly wrong — the realistic situation. A standard caricature is the contaminated normal $(1-\varepsilon)N(0,1)+\varepsilon N(0,\sigma_c^2)$ with $\sigma_c$ large, which has the right shape in the middle and far too much mass in the tails. The mean's variance is a weighted average that the contamination dominates: $\mathrm{Var}=(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\sigma_c^2$, which at $\varepsilon=0.1,\sigma_c=10$ is $0.9+10=10.9$, nearly $11\times$ the uncontaminated value.
Simulation check — 10% contamination, $n=50$

Data $0.9\,N(0,1)+0.1\,N(0,100)$, true centre $0$, $60{,}000$ reps:

  mean        : MSE = 0.21873   sd = 0.46769
  median      : MSE = 0.03745   sd = 0.19352
  20% trimmed : MSE = 0.02939   sd = 0.17144

(with no contamination, the mean's MSE would be 1/n = 0.0200)

The mean's MSE is inflated by a factor of $11$, exactly as the variance calculation predicts, while the median and trimmed mean are barely affected. Note the reversal against §10.1.3: under clean normality the median was $36\%$ worse than the mean; under $10\%$ contamination it is almost $6\times$ better. Choosing the mean is an implicit bet that the tails are exactly right.

§10.2.2 M-Estimators

Definition. Given a function $\psi$, an M-estimator $\hat\theta_M$ solves $$\sum_{i=1}^n\psi\big(X_i-\hat\theta_M\big)=0 .$$ Taking $\psi(u)=u$ gives the mean; $\psi(u)=\mathrm{sign}(u)$ gives the median; $\psi=\partial_\theta\log f$ gives the MLE. Huber's choice interpolates between the first two: $$\psi_k(u)=\max\{-k,\ \min\{k,\ u\}\},$$ linear in the middle and bounded outside, so a single wild observation has bounded influence.
Theorem (asymptotic variance of an M-estimator). Under regularity, with $\theta_0$ the solution of $E_{\theta_0}\psi(X-\theta_0)=0$, $$\sqrt n\big(\hat\theta_M-\theta_0\big)\xrightarrow{d}N\!\left(0,\;\frac{E\big[\psi(X-\theta_0)^2\big]}{\big(E\big[\psi'(X-\theta_0)\big]\big)^2}\right).$$

Proof.

The identical Taylor argument as for the MLE. Write $\Psi_n(\theta)=\sum_i\psi(X_i-\theta)$, so $\Psi_n(\hat\theta_M)=0$, and expand about $\theta_0$: $$0=\Psi_n(\theta_0)+(\hat\theta_M-\theta_0)\,\Psi_n'(\tilde\theta),$$ giving $$\sqrt n(\hat\theta_M-\theta_0)=\frac{\Psi_n(\theta_0)/\sqrt n}{-\Psi_n'(\tilde\theta)/n}.$$ The numerator is $n^{-1/2}\sum_i\psi(X_i-\theta_0)$, an average of iid mean-zero terms, so the CLT gives $N\big(0,E[\psi^2]\big)$. The denominator is an average converging in probability to $-E[\psi'(X-\theta_0)]\cdot(-1)=E[\psi']$ by the WLLN plus consistency. Slutsky finishes. ∎
Compare the MLE: there $\psi=$ score, and the two information identities make $E[\psi^2]=E[\psi']=I_1$, so the ratio collapses to $1/I_1$. For any other $\psi$ the numerator and denominator are genuinely different and the variance is strictly larger at the model — the precise price of robustness.
Simulation check — where Huber's $k=1.345$ comes from

For $\psi_k$ at the standard normal, $E[\psi_k']=2\Phi(k)-1$ and $E[\psi_k^2]=2\Phi(k)-1-2k\varphi(k)+2k^2(1-\Phi(k))$:

              n=100: n*var(Huber)=1.0568 (limit 1.0526)  n*var(mean)=1.0068 (limit 1)

The constant $1.345$ is reverse-engineered to give exactly $95\%$ efficiency at the normal — the computed ARE is $0.9500$ — and under contamination Huber beats both the mean ($7.5\times$) and the median. That is the entire design goal of robust statistics: give up $5\%$ at the ideal model to buy a large margin against the model being wrong.

§10.3 Hypothesis Testing

§10.3.1 Asymptotic Distribution of LRTs

Theorem (Wilks). For testing $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$ against $H_1:\theta\ne\theta_0$ in a one-parameter regular model, under $H_0$ $$-2\log\lambda(X)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\chi^2_1 .$$ More generally, if $H_0$ constrains $r$ free parameters out of $p$, the limit is $\chi^2_r$.

Proof (the one-parameter case).

Since $\hat\theta$ maximizes $\ell_n$ and $\lambda=L(\theta_0)/L(\hat\theta)$, $$-2\log\lambda=2\big[\ell_n(\hat\theta)-\ell_n(\theta_0)\big].$$ Taylor-expand $\ell_n(\theta_0)$ about $\hat\theta$, using $\ell_n'(\hat\theta)=0$: $$\ell_n(\theta_0)=\ell_n(\hat\theta)+\tfrac12(\theta_0-\hat\theta)^2\ell_n''(\tilde\theta)+\cdots,$$ so $$-2\log\lambda=-(\hat\theta-\theta_0)^2\,\ell_n''(\tilde\theta),$$ which factors as $$\underbrace{\big[\sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta_0)\big]^2}_{\xrightarrow{d}\;N(0,1/I_1)^2}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\frac{-\ell_n''(\tilde\theta)}{n}}_{\xrightarrow{P}\;I_1(\theta_0)} .$$ By §10.1.2 the first factor converges in distribution to the square of a $N(0,1/I_1)$ variable and the second in probability to $I_1$. By continuous mapping and Slutsky the product converges to $$I_1\cdot\frac{1}{I_1}Z^2=Z^2\sim\chi^2_1,\qquad Z\sim N(0,1). \qquad\blacksquare$$
Why this result is the workhorse of applied modelling. It requires no closed-form sampling distribution — only that you can maximize the likelihood twice, once under the constraint and once without. That is why "compare the deviances and look it up in a $\chi^2_r$ table" is the standard way to test nested logistic regressions, GLMs, and mixed models. The degrees of freedom $r$ counts constraints, which is also why it breaks when the constrained parameter sits on a boundary (variance components at zero), where the limit becomes a mixture of $\chi^2$'s rather than a $\chi^2$.

§10.3.2 Other Large-Sample Tests

Three statistics with the same limit. For $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$: $$\begin{aligned}\text{Wald:}&\quad W=\frac{(\hat\theta-\theta_0)^2}{1/\big(nI_1(\hat\theta)\big)},\\ \text{Score:}&\quad S=\frac{\big[\ell_n'(\theta_0)\big]^2}{nI_1(\theta_0)},\\ \text{LRT:}&\quad -2\log\lambda .\end{aligned}$$ All three converge in distribution to $\chi^2_1$ under $H_0$ (the first two by §10.1.2 and the CLT on the score respectively, the third by Wilks), so asymptotically they are equivalent.
The one structural difference — and it is the whole story. Wald evaluates the variance at the estimate $\hat\theta$; the score statistic evaluates it at the hypothesized $\theta_0$. Under $H_0$ the score version uses the correct variance by construction, whereas Wald uses an estimate that is itself noisy and, worse, degenerate at the boundary: for a Bernoulli sample with $x=0$, $\hat p(1-\hat p)=0$ and the Wald statistic is $0/0$. Score tests also require no fitting of the alternative model, which is why they are preferred for screening many hypotheses.

Excluding that, Wald* is not uniformly liberal but erratic, from $0.0003$ to $0.116$. At $p_0=0.5$ all three agree exactly. The reason is that each statistic is a strictly increasing function of $|x-n/2|$ there (for Wald, $|\hat p-\tfrac12|$ increases while $\hat p(1-\hat p)$ decreases, so the ratio increases), hence every rejection region has the form $\{|x-n/2|\ge c\}$; at these $n$ the three thresholds happen to select the same integer $c$. That is a coincidence of this symmetric case, not a general identity.

§10.4 Interval Estimation

§10.4.1 Approximate Maximum Likelihood Intervals

The construction, and its three variants. Inverting each test of §10.3.2 (by the duality of §9.2.1) gives an approximate $1-\alpha$ interval: $$\begin{aligned}\text{Wald:}&\quad \hat\theta\pm\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n\,I_1(\hat\theta)}},\\ \text{Score:}&\quad \Big\{\theta_0:\;\big[\ell_n'(\theta_0)\big]^2\le z^2_{1-\alpha/2}\,nI_1(\theta_0)\Big\},\\ \text{LRT:}&\quad \big\{\theta_0:\;-2\log\lambda(\theta_0)\le\chi^2_{1-\alpha,1}\big\}.\end{aligned}$$ Only the first has a closed form, which is exactly why it is the one in every textbook — and, as Chapter 9 found, the one that fails.
Proposition (the Wilson interval is the inverted score test). For $X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$, inverting the score test gives $$\frac{\hat p+\frac{z^2}{2n}\;\pm\;z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}},$$ which is the Wilson interval of §9.3.1.

Proof.

The score test accepts $p_0$ iff $n(\hat p-p_0)^2\le z^2p_0(1-p_0)$. Expanding and collecting powers of $p_0$: $$p_0^2(n+z^2)-p_0\big(2n\hat p+z^2\big)+n\hat p^2\le0 .$$ This is an upward parabola in $p_0$, so the acceptance set is the interval between its roots. The quadratic formula gives roots $$p_0=\frac{2n\hat p+z^2\pm\sqrt{(2n\hat p+z^2)^2-4(n+z^2)n\hat p^2}}{2(n+z^2)},$$ and the discriminant simplifies as $$(2n\hat p+z^2)^2-4(n+z^2)n\hat p^2=z^2\big(4n\hat p(1-\hat p)+z^2\big).$$ Substituting and dividing numerator and denominator by $2n$ yields the displayed form. ∎
This closes the loop from Chapter 9. Chapter 9 observed empirically that Wilson works and Wald does not, without explaining why. The reason is now exact: Wilson is the score interval, and the score statistic uses $p_0(1-p_0)$ — the variance that is correct under the hypothesis being tested — whereas Wald uses $\hat p(1-\hat p)$, which is noisy and collapses to zero at $x=0$ or $x=n$. One choice of where to evaluate a variance accounts for the entire difference between a usable interval and one with minimum coverage $0.0198$.

Simulation check — four intervals, exact coverage

$n=20$, nominal $95\%$. The identity above verified numerically, then exact coverage by summation over $p$:

  method            |  min cov  |  mean cov  |  mean length
  Wald              |  0.0198   |   0.8467   |    0.3243
  Score (Wilson)    |  0.8516   |   0.9532   |    0.3255

The identity holds to machine precision. On coverage, Score and LRT-inverted are both acceptable and cost essentially nothing in length relative to Wald; Clopper–Pearson buys its guarantee with $13\%$ more length; Wald is simply broken. Note that mean coverage alone would rank Wald at $0.847$ — it is the minimum over $p$ that exposes the failure, which is a general lesson about evaluating approximate procedures.

Flag — what "asymptotically valid" does and does not buy you. All four intervals above are asymptotically correct: each one's coverage tends to $0.95$ as $n\to\infty$ for fixed $p\in(0,1)$. That statement is true of the Wald interval too, and it is nearly worthless, because the convergence is not uniform in $p$ — for any $n$ there are values of $p$ near the boundary where coverage is terrible. This is the single most important caveat in the chapter: an asymptotic justification is a limit statement at fixed parameter value, and real data come with a fixed $n$ and a possibly awkward parameter. Always check finite-sample behaviour over the whole parameter range you might actually encounter, as the exact-coverage computations here do.

Where this leaves you

The debts are paid. The MLE is asymptotically normal with variance equal to the Cramér–Rao bound — proved from the CLT on the score, the WLLN on the curvature, and Slutsky, with the two information identities supplying the cancellation that makes it efficient — and the delta method extends that efficiency to every smooth function of $\theta$. Wilks's theorem gives $-2\log\lambda\to\chi^2_r$, which is what makes likelihood-based testing possible in models with no closed-form distribution theory. And the Wald/score/LRT trio, identical in the limit, separates in practice by where the variance is evaluated, which is precisely the resolution of Chapter 9's Wald puzzle.

The counterweight is just as important. Asymptotic efficiency did not make the MLE beat the UMVUE at $n=5$. The $\chi^2_1$ approximation is governed by $np_0$, not $n$. The median loses $36\%$ to the mean under normality and wins by $6\times$ under $10\%$ contamination, so efficiency comparisons are claims about tails. The bootstrap gives standard errors for functionals with no formula, and converges to the wrong law for a maximum. Chapters 11 and 12 now apply all of this to linear and nonlinear models, where the exact normal-theory results of Theorem 5.3.1 return and the asymptotics of this chapter take over wherever they run out.

8.3.2 NEYMAN-PEARSON: the LR test beats every competitor of the same size
========================================================================
  Simple vs simple: H0: mu=0 vs H1: mu=0.5, sigma=1 known, n=10, alpha=0.05.
  The likelihood ratio is monotone in Xbar, so the NP test rejects for large Xbar.
  critical values: Xbar > 0.5201 | median > 0.6104 (calibrated) | X1 > 1.6449
  test        |  achieved size  |  power at mu=0.5   | analytic power
  Xbar (NP)   |     0.0500      |      0.4757        |    0.4746
  median      |     0.0506      |      0.3834        |       --  
  X1 only     |     0.0500      |      0.1258        |    0.1261
  -> all three have size 0.05; the NP (Xbar) test has the largest power, as the lemma requires.

8.3.2 NO UMP TEST EXISTS for the two-sided problem: the power curves cross
========================================================================
  one-sided test: Xbar > 0.5201    two-sided test: |Xbar| > 0.6198   (both size 0.05)
   mu    |  one-sided power  |  two-sided power
  -1.00  |      0.0000       |      0.8860
  -0.50  |      0.0007       |      0.3527
  -0.20  |      0.0111       |      0.0968
   0.00  |      0.0496       |      0.0490
   0.20  |      0.1569       |      0.0982
   0.50  |      0.4737       |      0.3524
   1.00  |      0.9346       |      0.8851
  -> neither dominates: one-sided wins for mu>0, two-sided wins for mu<0. So in the
     two-sided problem no single test is uniformly most powerful.

========================================================================
8.3.3 IUT: an intersection-union test has size <= alpha, attained only in a limit
========================================================================
  H0: mu1<=0 OR mu2<=0   vs   H1: both > 0.  Reject iff BOTH z-tests reject at 0.05.
  (mu1,mu2)=( 0.0, 0.0) in H0: rejection probability = 0.0027
  (mu1,mu2)=( 0.0, 0.5) in H0: rejection probability = 0.0235
  (mu1,mu2)=( 0.0, 1.0) in H0: rejection probability = 0.0470
  (mu1,mu2)=( 0.0, 3.0) in H0: rejection probability = 0.0505
  (mu1,mu2)=(-0.5, 3.0) in H0: rejection probability = 0.0007
  -> at (0,0) it is about alpha^2=0.0025; it climbs to alpha=0.05 only as the
     second mean runs off to infinity. The size is alpha but the test is conservative inside H0.

========================================================================
8.2.1 THE LRT FOR A NORMAL MEAN IS THE t-TEST: lambda = (1 + t^2/(n-1))^(-n/2)
========================================================================
  max |lambda - (1+t^2/(n-1))^(-n/2)| over 100000 random samples = 3.664e-15
  -> an exact algebraic identity, so 'reject when lambda <= c' IS 'reject when |t| >= c''.

========================================================================
8.3.1 DISCRETE TESTS CANNOT HIT A NOMINAL SIZE: Binomial(20,p), H0: p=0.5 vs H1: p>0.5
========================================================================
   k  |  P(X >= k | p=0.5)
   12 |      0.25172
   13 |      0.13159
   14 |      0.05766
   15 |      0.02069
   16 |      0.00591
   17 |      0.00129
  smallest k with P(X>=k|0.5) <= 0.05 is k=15, giving achieved size 0.02069
  the nominal 0.05 is unreachable: k=14 would give 0.05766 > 0.05.
  simulated size at p=0.5: 0.02069   (matches the exact 0.02069)
  -> real size 0.021, not 0.05: the test spends only 41% of its error budget, and loses power accordingly.

========================================================================
8.2.2 BAYESIAN TESTS: a p-value is NOT P(H0 | data)  (Jeffreys-Lindley)
========================================================================
  H0: mu=0 vs H1: mu!=0, sigma=1 known, prior P(H0)=0.5, and mu ~ N(0,tau^2) under H1.
  Observe exactly z = 1.95996, i.e. a two-sided p-value of 0.05, for each n.
      n   |  two-sided p  |  Bayes factor B01  |  P(H0 | data)
       10 |     0.0500    |        0.5786      |     0.3665
      100 |     0.0500    |        1.5006      |     0.6001
     1000 |     0.0500    |        4.6440      |     0.8228
    10000 |     0.0500    |       14.6536      |     0.9361
   100000 |     0.0500    |       46.3285      |     0.9789
  -> at n=10 a p-value of 0.05 leaves P(H0|data) about 0.37, not 0.05; by n=100000 the
     SAME p-value is strong evidence FOR H0 (P(H0|data)=0.98). The p-value and the
     posterior probability of H0 answer different questions and can point opposite ways.

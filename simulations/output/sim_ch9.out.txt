9.3.1 CONDITIONAL COVERAGE: a 95% interval that is sometimes 100%, sometimes under 60%
========================================================================
  n=5: the half-width giving 95% UNCONDITIONAL coverage is c = 0.22536
  check: computed unconditional coverage at that c = 0.9500
  conditional coverage is exactly 1 as soon as r >= 1-2c = 0.5493
   range R bin     |  share of samples |  cond. coverage |  theory (bin average)
   [0.0000,0.3000) |      0.0306       |     0.5945      |      0.5931
   [0.3000,0.5493) |      0.2242       |     0.8330      |      0.8330
   [0.5493,0.7800) |      0.4408       |     1.0000      |      1.0000
   [0.7800,1.0000) |      0.3043       |     1.0000      |      1.0000
  -> the procedure has exactly 95% coverage overall, yet conditional on a wide observed
     range it covers with probability 1, and on a narrow range far less. Averaging over
     samples you did not get is doing real work here (cf. ancillarity, Chapter 6).

9.3.4 LOSS FUNCTIONS: trading length against coverage
========================================================================
  Interval xbar +/- c/sqrt(n) for a normal mean, sigma=1 known, n=10.
  Loss = length + k*(1 - coverage) = 2c/sqrt(n) + k*(2 - 2*Phi(c)).
  Stationarity: d/dc = 2/sqrt(n) - 2k*phi(c) = 0, i.e. phi(c) = 1/(k*sqrt(n)).
     k    |  optimal c  |  implied coverage  |  loss at optimum
     2.00 |   1.3605    |      0.8263        |     1.2078
     5.00 |   1.9193    |      0.9450        |     1.4886
     5.41 |   1.9601    |      0.9500        |     1.5102
    10.00 |   2.2516    |      0.9757        |     1.6675
    50.00 |   2.8790    |      0.9960        |     2.0203
  -> the conventional c=1.96 (95%) is the optimum for k=5.4106:
     choosing '95%' is implicitly pricing a unit of non-coverage at that many units of length.
